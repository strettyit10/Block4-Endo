L1 — Hypothalamic-Pituitary Hormone Relationships I

Endocrine Gland Classification

Hypothalamus & Pituitary Anatomy

  • Hypothalamus = part of the diencephalon; sits superior to the pituitary in the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone
  • Connected by the infundibulum (pituitary stalk)
  • Anterior pituitary = adenohypophysis (glandular tissue; "pars distalis")
  • Posterior pituitary = neurohypophysis (neural tissue; axon terminals from hypothalamic neurons)
  • Pars intermedia: involutes in adults (degenerates; present in fetal/early childhood)

Hypophyseal Portal System

Hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones enter the first capillary network in the median eminence, travel via portal vessels down the infundibulum, and reach the second capillary bed in the anterior pituitary. This is the superior hypophyseal artery system.

  • Median eminence: area where blood-brain barrier is weakened → hormones can enter portal bloodstream
  • Neurohypophysis has a separate blood supply (inferior hypophyseal artery)
  • Circumventricular organs (OVLT, median eminence, neurohypophysis) = "windows through the BBB" for sensing blood composition

⭐ High-Yield
  • Prolactin is the ONLY anterior pituitary hormone under exclusively inhibitory hypothalamic control. All others have at least one releasing hormone. PIH = dopamine (a small molecule, unlike all other hypothalamic hormones which are peptides).
  • Cut the infundibulum (ligate the stalk) → prolactin rises (dopamine inhibition removed), all others fall (lose their releasing hormones).
  • Pars intermedia produces MSH in fetal life; in adults, MSH is produced mainly by ACTH cleavage from POMC.
⚠️ Common Traps
  • Anterior pituitary ≠ directly neural. It is glandular (adenohypophysis). The posterior pituitary is neural (axon terminals).
  • The pancreas is NOT under hypothalamic-pituitary control — it self-regulates via blood glucose.
  • TRH stimulates both TSH and prolactin secretion from the anterior pituitary.
🧪 Practice Question
A researcher ligates the infundibular stalk in a rat, cutting portal blood flow to the anterior pituitary. Twenty-four hours later, which hormone is most likely to be elevated in serum?
A. TSH
B. FSH
C. Prolactin ✓
D. GH
E. ACTH
Answer: C. Prolactin is the only anterior pituitary hormone under exclusively inhibitory hypothalamic control (via dopamine/PIH). Cutting the stalk removes dopaminergic inhibition → prolactin rises. All other anterior pituitary hormones lose their releasing-hormone signals and fall.

Pulsatile Secretion

  • Ultrashort-loop autoinhibition creates pulsatile secretion patterns
  • Prevents target cell desensitization/habituation (constant hormone = no new signal = receptor downregulation)
  • GnRH must be pulsatile — continuous GnRH → pituitary desensitization → ↓LH/FSH (used therapeutically as Leuprolide for prostate cancer, endometriosis)
  • Pulsatile patterns are superimposed on ultradian and circadian rhythms

Positive Feedback (Exception to the Rule)

  • Most hormonal axes use negative feedback. Key exceptions:
  • Estradiol surge → LH surge (ovulation): Gradually rising [estradiol] over the follicular phase switches GnRH regulation from negative to positive feedback → LH surge → ovulation
  • Oxytocin in labor: Uterine contractions stimulate more oxytocin release (Ferguson reflex) → contractions intensify until delivery
  • Similar positive feedback drives ejaculation in males

⭐ High-Yield
  • "GH acts fast, anabolic; when fasting, GH spares glucose." Dual roles of GH are a core exam concept (see L4).
  • Prolactin: causes milk production. Oxytocin: causes milk ejection (let-down reflex). Don't confuse these.
  • Sudden unexplained hyperpigmentation → think excess ACTH → cleaved to produce excess MSH → skin darkening.
📝 How This Is Tested
  • Histology question: identify anterior vs posterior pituitary on H&E; anterior = densely cellular (purple dots), posterior = sparse glial cells + Herring bodies
  • Feedback loop questions: know which hormone feeds back to which level (long, short, ultrashort)
  • Positive feedback: ovulation LH surge mechanism; why GnRH agonists cause initial flare then suppression

L3 — Posterior Pituitary Hormones: Vasopressin & Oxytocin

Structure of AVP & OXT

  • Both are nonapeptides (9 amino acids)
  • Both have a cyclic disulfide bond at the N-terminus (protects from aminopeptidases) and an amide group at C-terminus (protects from carboxypeptidases)
  • Differ by only 2 amino acids (positions 3 and 8)
  • Synthesized in magnocellular neurons of the SON and PVN of the hypothalamus
  • Transported down axons → stored as Herring bodies in posterior pituitary → released into fenestrated capillaries

Key Thresholds

  • Osmotic threshold for AVP secretion: ~280 mOsm/kg (linear relationship above this)
  • Osmotic threshold for thirst: ~5–10 mOsm/kg above the AVP threshold
  • Maximal antidiuresis at AVP ≈ 5 pg/mL; additional AVP above this does not further concentrate urine
  • As MAP ↑, the osmotic threshold for AVP rises → harder to stimulate AVP when BP is elevated

Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

Definition: Large volumes of dilute urine that cannot be reduced by water restriction. Serum = hypernatremia. Urine = hypotonic.

DDAVP (Desmopressin) — Key Properties

  • 1-desamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin: structural modifications = deaminated at position 1, D-arginine at position 8
  • Selectively binds V2R only (no V1a binding → no vasoconstriction, no BP effects)
  • Longer half-life than AVP (deaminated residue protects from degradation)
  • Administered intranasally (bypasses first-pass metabolism, highly vascularized nasal mucosa, peptide-safe)
  • Resistant to vasopressinase → effective in gestational DI

Oxytocin

  • Receptor couples to Gq → PLC → IP3 → Ca²⁺ in uterus and breast
  • Also activates PLA2 in uterus → prostaglandins → cervical ripening and pliability
  • Milk ejection (let-down): suckling → sensory input → hypothalamus → OT release → myoepithelial cells contract → milk ejected into ducts
  • Labor contractions: Uterine OT receptors only expressed near term (prevent premature contractions). OT drives contractions → positive feedback (Ferguson reflex) → more OT
  • Tocolysis with atosiban (OT antagonist) is not effective — OT plays only a minor role in initiating labor
  • Behavioral roles: pair bonding, social recognition, memory, metabolism
⭐ High-Yield
  • DI = hypernatremia + dilute urine + polydipsia/polyuria. Distinguishing central from nephrogenic: water deprivation test + response to DDAVP.
  • Lithium → nephrogenic DI (blocks V2R signaling). Up to 20% of lithium users affected.
  • Alcohol → ↓ ADH → diuresis — classic trap: ethanol suppresses AVP.
  • Oxytocin = milk ejection (let-down). Prolactin = milk production. These are different mechanisms and different cells.
⚠️ Common Traps
  • DI → hypernatremia, not hyponatremia. You are losing free water → serum sodium concentrates. SIADH = hyponatremia (too much water retained).
  • Nephrogenic DI has high ADH (kidney can't respond); central DI has low ADH.
  • DDAVP is selective for V2R (not V1a) → no vasoconstriction; safe for long-term use in DI without BP changes.
🧪 Practice Question
A 35-year-old man presents with polyuria and polydipsia. Labs show serum Na⁺ 152 mEq/L, serum osmolality 305 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 90 mOsm/kg. After water deprivation, urine osmolality increases to 580 mOsm/kg. After DDAVP administration, urine osmolality increases to 700 mOsm/kg. Which diagnosis best fits?
A. Central diabetes insipidus ✓
B. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
C. SIADH
D. Primary polydipsia
Answer: A. Hypernatremia + dilute urine = DI. Response to DDAVP (urine concentrates further) = kidneys are intact → central DI (can't make ADH). Nephrogenic DI: kidneys would NOT respond to DDAVP. Primary polydipsia: serum osmolality would be LOW (too much water), not elevated.

L4 — Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Growth Hormone Axis

GH Axis Overview

LevelSignalFeedback to Hypothalamus
HypothalamusGHRH (stimulates) / Somatostatin (inhibits)Ultrashort loop (GHRH auto-inhibition)
Anterior pituitaryGH released from somatotropesShort-loop: GH → inhibits GHRH, stimulates SS
Liver (primary target)GH → IGF-1 (somatomedin C) releaseLong-loop: IGF-1 → inhibits GHRH, stimulates SS; also inhibits GH from pituitary
  • GH = 191-aa protein; secreted from somatotropes in the anterior pituitary
  • 50% of GH circulates bound to GHBP (growth hormone-binding protein) → ↑ half-life (~20 min)
  • IGF-1 = 70-aa peptide; almost entirely bound to IGFBP (IGF-binding protein) → half-life ~12 hours
  • IGFBP proteases cleave IGFBP to release and activate IGF-1
  • GH high homology to prolactin → can bind PRL receptor at high concentrations

Dual Roles of GH

StateGH ActionTarget TissuesMetabolic Effect
Fed state (↑ AA)Protein anabolismMuscle, liver, adipose↑ Protein synthesis; ↑ glucose uptake (insulin-like)
Fasting state (↓ glucose)Glucose-sparing effectMuscle, adipose↓ Glucose uptake (insulin antagonism); ↑ lipolysis; ↑ gluconeogenesis (liver)
  • GH release stimulated by high serum [AA]; GH release inhibited by high serum [glucose]
  • Insulin is required for GH → IGF-1 axis: Insulin drives GH receptor expression in liver → without insulin, liver cannot respond to GH to secrete IGF-1. Balanced meal = growth.
  • A carb-heavy, protein-light meal → energy storage (insulin driven) but NOT growth

The Diabetogenic Effect of GH

When GH is chronically elevated, its glucose-sparing effects in muscle and adipose induce insulin resistance. Serum [glucose] and [FFAs] rise → ↑ incidence of diabetes mellitus. Disorders of excess GH secretion (gigantism, acromegaly) are associated with DM.

⭐ High-Yield
  • GH is anabolic when fed, glucose-sparing when fasting. This dual role and its interaction with insulin are extremely high-yield.
  • Laron syndrome = ↑ GH but ↓ IGF-1 (receptor problem). Classic trap: mistaken for GH deficiency because phenotype is similar.
  • Acromegaly: think pituitary adenoma in an adult. Bitemporal hemianopsia = optic chiasm compression. Almost always a prolactinoma or GH-secreting adenoma (check visual fields in any pituitary mass).
  • Fetal growth is NOT GH-dependent — hPL (human placental lactogen / hCS) from the placenta drives intrauterine growth.
⚠️ Common Traps
  • Insulin is required for GH to signal through the liver. In insulin-deficient states (T1DM), GH can't drive IGF-1 → poor growth despite normal GH secretion.
  • GH deficiency in adults: surprisingly well-tolerated (sex steroids/other hormones compensate). Most detrimental during childhood.
  • Chronic hypercortisolemia (Cushing's) inhibits GH — opposite of what physiological cortisol does.
🧪 Practice Question
A 12-year-old boy is evaluated for short stature. Serum GH level is markedly elevated. IGF-1 level is markedly decreased. He does not respond to recombinant GH therapy. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
A. GH-secreting pituitary adenoma
B. Pituitary dwarfism
C. Laron syndrome ✓
D. Constitutional growth delay
E. Hypothyroidism
Answer: C. Laron syndrome = GH receptor (or post-receptor signaling) mutation. GH is made in excess (trying to compensate) but cannot signal → no IGF-1 production. Phenotypically similar to GH deficiency (dwarfism) but with HIGH GH. Recombinant GH won't help — needs recombinant IGF-1.

⚠️ Trap — Euthyroid Sick Syndrome (Non-Thyroidal Illness)

In severe illness (sepsis, surgery, fasting), Type I deiodinase is downregulated and Type III is upregulated → ↓ T3, ↑ reverse T3 (rT3), normal or low TSH. The patient is NOT hypothyroid — this is an adaptive response to reduce metabolic rate during critical illness. Do NOT treat with T3/T4 — TSH is the key discriminator. If TSH is normal/low, this is not primary hypothyroidism.

⭐ TBG Changes — The Total vs. Free Trap
  • ↑ TBG (pregnancy, oral contraceptives, estrogen therapy) → ↑ total T4, but free T4 is NORMAL → patient is euthyroid. TSH is normal.
  • ↓ TBG (androgens, nephrotic syndrome, liver failure, high-dose glucocorticoids) → ↓ total T4, but free T4 is NORMAL → patient is euthyroid. TSH is normal.
  • Test of choice to assess thyroid function: TSH first. Then free T4 if TSH is abnormal. Total T4 is misleading.

Negative Feedback & Axis Regulation

  • T3 is the primary feedback signal — T3 (not T4) binds thyroid hormone receptors (TR) in both hypothalamus and anterior pituitary to suppress TRH and TSH.
  • The pituitary is MORE sensitive to T3 feedback than the hypothalamus because thyrotropes have high local Type II deiodinase activity → convert intracellular T4→T3 efficiently.
  • TSH is the most sensitive indicator of axis function: small changes in free T4 cause large, inverse changes in TSH (log-linear relationship).
  • Somatostatin and dopamine also inhibit TSH secretion (clinically relevant during critical illness).

📝 How This Is Tested
  • Given a TSH + T4 → identify primary vs. secondary vs. subclinical. TSH is always the first-line test.
  • Pregnant patient with elevated total T4 but normal TSH → euthyroid (↑ TBG from estrogen). Do NOT treat.

  • Mechanism of Graves disease: TSI (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins) bind and activate TSHR like TSH → autonomous stimulation → hyperthyroidism + low TSH.
  • Cretinism prevention: iodine supplementation in pregnancy + universal newborn thyroid screening.
🧪 Practice Question
A 32-year-old woman who is 14 weeks pregnant has a routine TSH of 1.8 mIU/L (normal) but a total T4 of 18 µg/dL (elevated; normal 5–12). She has no symptoms of thyroid disease. What is the most likely explanation?
A. Graves disease — TSIs stimulating the thyroid
B. Subclinical hyperthyroidism — treat with propylthiouracil
C. Elevated TBG from estrogen → increased total T4, normal free T4 ✓
D. hCG cross-reacting with TSHR → driving thyroid hormone production
Answer: C. Pregnancy → high estrogen → ↑ hepatic TBG synthesis → more T4 is protein-bound → total T4 rises, but free T4 is UNCHANGED → patient is euthyroid. TSH is normal, confirming this. The key rule: total T4 can be misleading; always interpret with TSH and free T4. Note: hCG does have weak TSHR activity and can transiently lower TSH in early pregnancy — but that would cause low TSH, not the picture here.

The SAM Axis (Sympathetic-Adrenal-Medullary)

  • Pre-autonomic neurons from PVN → preganglionic neurons → splanchnic nerve → adrenal chromaffin cells (modified postganglionic neurons)
  • Chromaffin cells release epinephrine (75%) + norepinephrine (25%) into blood
  • Time to peak effect: ~30 seconds; returns to baseline within minutes if stimulus stops

The HPA Axis

  • CRH (41 aa, from PVN of hypothalamus) → ACTH (39 aa, from corticotropes of anterior pituitary) → Cortisol (from zona fasciculata)
  • ACTH is cleaved from POMC (proopiomelanocortin); co-products: MSH, β-endorphin, β-lipotropin
  • ACTH also drives androgen production (zona reticularis) and is permissive for mineralocorticoid synthesis, but does NOT directly trigger aldosterone release
  • Vasopressin (co-released with CRH) enhances ACTH secretion
  • ACTH stimulates LDL uptake and cholesterol → pregnenolone conversion in zona fasciculata

Cortisol Actions

Cortisol Circadian Rhythm

  • Cortisol peaks at waking (~6–8 AM), then declines throughout the day (with pulsatile superimposition)
  • Pre-waking rise: ↑ CRH → ↑ ACTH → ↑ cortisol → prepares body for the "metabolic demands of the day" (gluconeogenesis after overnight fast)
  • Cortisol can bind both GR (glucocorticoid receptor) and MR (mineralocorticoid receptor)
  • 11β-HSD2 in aldosterone-sensitive tissues converts cortisol → cortisone (inactive) to prevent cortisol from flooding MRs → MR specificity for aldosterone

Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)

Autosomal recessive enzymatic defects in adrenal steroidogenesis. Most common (90%): 21-hydroxylase deficiency.

FeatureDetails
Enzyme blocked21-hydroxylase (CYP21A2) — cannot convert 17-OHP → 11-deoxycortisol (cortisol pathway) OR progesterone → 11-deoxycorticosterone (aldosterone pathway)

⚠️ CAH vs. Addison's — Key Distinction
  • Both: ↑↑ ACTH + ↓ cortisol
  • CAH has: ↑ 17-OHP + androgen excess (virilization, ambiguous genitalia) — the distinguishing biochemical marker
  • Addison's has: normal or low androgens; no 17-OHP accumulation; often autoimmune etiology in adults
  • CAH presenting finding in a neonate: ambiguous genitalia (46,XX female) or unexplained salt-wasting crisis (46,XY male)
⭐ High-Yield
  • Addison's = ↑ ACTH → hyperpigmentation + ↓ Na⁺ + ↑ K⁺ + ↓ cortisol + ↓ aldosterone
  • Cushing's disease = ↑ ACTH from pituitary adenoma → hyperpigmentation + ↑ cortisol
  • Adrenal tumor causing Cushing's = ↓ ACTH (direct cortisol → suppresses pituitary) → NO hyperpigmentation
  • Cortisol has a permissive effect on vasopressors (upregulates adrenergic receptors). Adrenal crisis = refractory hypotension → give hydrocortisone emergently.
  • 11β-HSD2 deficiency → cortisol acts as a mineralocorticoid → hypertension + hypokalemia (a "secondary" form of hyperaldosteronism-like presentation with LOW aldosterone).
  • CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency) = ↑ ACTH + ↓ cortisol + ↓ aldosterone + ↑↑ 17-OHP + androgen excess → ambiguous genitalia (46,XX) or salt-wasting crisis (neonate).

B. Secondary adrenal insufficiency

Secondary adrenal insufficiency = pituitary failure → ↓ ACTH (no hyperpigmentation, no hyperkalemia). Cushing's = excess cortisol, opposite labs.

L6 — Stress, Stress Hormones & Pathophysiology Integration

Defining Stress & Stressors

  • Stressor: anything that disrupts homeostasis or is perceived as a threat
  • Stress response: nonspecific — body responds the same way regardless of stressor quality
  • Can be detected sensorially (conscious/cognitive) OR bypass cortical awareness and go directly to the hypothalamus/ANS

General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) — Selye's Model

StageNamePhysiologyClinical Analogy
1AlarmImmediate strong response; initial ↓ resistance; fight-or-flight activated (SAM axis); begins HPA axis activationAcute injury, sudden illness
2ResistanceBody adapts; ↑ resistance; hormonal tolerance; cortisol maintains metabolic needs; begins habituationSubacute illness, sustained work stress
3ExhaustionCannot maintain stress response; allostatic overload; tissue damage; organ dysfunctionBurnout, septic shock, prolonged starvation

Allostasis vs Homeostasis

  • Homeostasis: maintaining a fixed set point (classic thermostat model)
  • Allostasis: "stability through change" — adjusting set points and mobilizing resources to survive stressors. The body defends survival, not a fixed value.
  • Allostatic load: cumulative burden of all stressors over time; quantifies wear on the system

Reactive Scope Model (4 Zones)

ZoneDescriptionOutcome
1. Predictive homeostasisNormal, cyclical hormone range; body anticipates and prepares for known stressors (circadian, seasonal)Healthy baseline; energy efficient
2. Reactive homeostasisHormones rise outside normal range to handle unpredicted stressorsAdaptive; maps to alarm + resistance stages of GAS
3. Homeostatic overloadHormone levels exceed the reactive maximum; system is overwhelmedPathological; maps to late exhaustion (e.g., Takotsubo/broken heart syndrome from massive catecholamine surge)
4. Homeostatic failureHormone levels fall below predictive minimum; glands depleted/failedAdrenal crisis; septic shock; multiorgan failure

CRH as the Central Stress Integrator

CRH from the PVN does more than drive the HPA axis — it is the master coordinator of the integrated stress response:

  • HPA axis: CRH → anterior pituitary corticotropes → ACTH → cortisol (the classic pathway)
  • LC-NE system: CRH directly activates the locus coeruleus (LC) → ↑ norepinephrine release throughout the brain → arousal, vigilance, anxiety
  • HPG axis suppression: CRH increases GnIH (gonadotropin-inhibitory hormone) and directly suppresses GnRH pulsatility → inhibits reproduction
  • HPT axis suppression: CRH → cortisol → ↓ TSH secretion + ↓ peripheral T4→T3 conversion; reduces metabolic rate during stress
  • Appetite suppression: CRH in hypothalamus reduces food intake (anorexigenic effect) — one reason stress causes appetite loss

Pro-Inflammatory Cytokines & the Stress Response

  • During infection or tissue injury, macrophages/monocytes release IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α
  • These cytokines cross the blood-brain barrier (or signal via vagal afferents) and directly stimulate CRH release from the PVN → activates the HPA axis
  • This is the mechanism of "sickness behavior": fever, fatigue, anorexia, ↓ libido, ↓ activity — all mediated through CRH/cortisol/NE responses to cytokines
  • Cortisol then feeds back to suppress cytokine production (inhibits NF-κB → ↓ IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) — this is the anti-inflammatory mechanism and explains why glucocorticoids treat inflammatory diseases
  • Chronic inflammatory disease (RA, IBD, lupus) → persistent cytokine elevation → chronic HPA activation → HPA dysregulation and fatigue

Pathological Consequences of Chronic Stress

  • ↓ Reproductive function: Chronic cortisol + CRH → HPG axis suppression at every level → amenorrhea, ↓ libido, ↓ fertility
  • Osteoporosis: ↑ Cortisol → ↓ bone formation + ↑ resorption + ↑ renal Ca²⁺ loss
  • Metabolic syndrome: Insulin resistance, central adiposity (visceral fat = GC-sensitive), HTN, dyslipidemia
  • Immunosuppression: ↓ T-cell activity, ↓ antibody production → ↑ infection risk; ↓ NK cell surveillance
  • Hippocampal atrophy: Prolonged GC exposure → dendritic retraction, ↓ neurogenesis → cognitive impairment, depression, impaired fear extinction (PTSD)
  • Takotsubo syndrome (broken heart syndrome): Massive catecholamine surge → apical LV ballooning → transient cardiomyopathy; ECG changes mimic STEMI; normal coronaries
  • GI dysfunction: Chronic sympathetic activation + cortisol → ↓ GI motility, ↑ gut permeability, ↑ acid secretion → stress ulcers, worsening IBS/IBD. (Selye's original rats developed gastric ulcers — this was part of his original GAS discovery.)
  • Cancer and metastasis: Chronic immunosuppression (↓ NK cell activity) → impaired tumor surveillance; β-adrenergic signaling on tumor cells → ↑ VEGF, ↑ angiogenesis, ↑ metastatic potential. Epidemiological studies link chronic stress/depression with ↑ cancer incidence and worse outcomes.

Interventions that reduce allostatic load have measurable neuroendocrine effects:

⭐ High-Yield — Integration Summary
  • Acute stress → SAM (fast, EPI/NE) then HPA (slower, cortisol). Together: ↑ glucose, ↑ HR, ↑ BP, ↑ alertness. Classic fight-or-flight.
  • Chronic stress → pathology: Cushing's-like metabolic picture, immunosuppression, reproductive suppression, osteoporosis, hippocampal damage.
  • Cortisol feedback timing: cortisol levels rise → feed back to hypothalamus (CRH ↓) and pituitary (ACTH ↓) via long-loop feedback. This is a key test of the HPA axis integrity (cosyntropin stimulation test).
  • Predictability matters: Predictable stressors activate predictive homeostasis (lower allostatic load). Unpredictable stressors require reactive homeostasis (higher physiologic cost).
📝 How This Is Tested
  • Integration vignettes: student before exam (SAM first, then HPA) — which hormones rise, in what order?
  • Mechanism of stress-induced amenorrhea: chronic cortisol → ↑ GnIH + direct HPG inhibition → ↓ LH/FSH → anovulation
  • Chronic stress and immune suppression: decreased T-cell cytokines, initial antibody ↑ then sustained ↓
  • Distinguish SAM from HPA by onset, duration, and hormone identity
  • Allostasis: explains why the same hormone concentration can be "normal" in one context and pathological in another
🧪 Practice Question
A 24-year-old female medical student is about to give a presentation she has been dreading. Within 30 seconds of starting, her heart is pounding and her palms are sweating. Two hours later, she still feels anxious and notices her appetite is suppressed. Which of the following best explains her presentation-day symptoms at the 2-hour mark?
A. Epinephrine secretion from the adrenal medulla
B. Direct sympathetic activation of sweat glands
C. Cortisol-mediated gluconeogenesis and appetite suppression ✓
D. Vasopressin-mediated water retention
E. Oxytocin-mediated social bonding
Answer: C. The 30-second symptoms (racing heart, sweating) reflect the fast SAM axis (EPI/NE). By 2 hours, catecholamine effects have resolved; what persists is HPA axis activation → elevated cortisol → ↑ gluconeogenesis, ↓ appetite (cortisol inhibits digestive/reproductive functions). This illustrates the temporal sequence: SAM (seconds) → HPA (minutes to hours).
