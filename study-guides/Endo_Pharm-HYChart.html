Pituitary — Dopamine Agonists & Somatostatin Analogs

Cabergoline · Bromocriptine Dopamine Agonists — D2 Receptor PO · Cabergoline: 2×/week · Bromocriptine: daily
⚙ MOA
Activate D2 receptors on lactotrophs → ↓ prolactin synthesis and secretion. Dopamine normally tonically inhibits prolactin release from the anterior pituitary.

Cabergoline: D2-selective, longer t½, better tolerated.
Bromocriptine: non-selective D2/D1, shorter t½, requires daily dosing.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Nausea/vomiting (take with food)
Orthostatic hypotension (1st dose — start low)
Headache, dizziness
Impulse control disorders (cabergoline — esp. at high doses)
Cardiac valvulopathy (cabergoline at very high doses — monitor echo)

Monitor prolactin + MRI to assess tumor response.
🎯 Clinical Uses
Prolactinoma — 1st lineHyperprolactinemiaAcromegaly (adjunct)

Cabergoline shrinks tumor AND normalizes prolactin. Even macroadenomas respond. Surgery reserved for drug failure/intolerance or rapid visual loss.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Cabergoline = 1st line for prolactinoma (preferred over bromocriptine — twice weekly, better tolerated, higher efficacy).
Shrinks tumor — even macroadenomas. Surgery rarely needed for prolactinoma.
Bromocriptine: also used for type 2 DM (Cycloset) — improves insulin sensitivity via CNS dopamine mechanism.
Mild prolactin elevation (<150 ng/mL) + large sellar mass = stalk compression, NOT prolactinoma. A true macro-prolactinoma causing 2+ cm mass → prolactin usually >500 ng/mL. Don't start cabergoline on a craniopharyngioma.
Octreotide · Lanreotide · Pasireotide Somatostatin Analogs — SSTR Agonists SC or IM depot (monthly) · Monitor glucose, gallbladder US
⚙ MOA
Bind somatostatin receptors (SSTR1–5) → inhibit GH secretion (somatotrophs), ACTH (corticotrophs — pasireotide), TSH, glucagon, insulin, gut hormones (gastrin, secretin, VIP).

Octreotide/Lanreotide: SSTR2 > 5 (primarily GH/IGF-1 suppression).
Pasireotide: pan-SSTR (1,2,3,5) → also suppresses ACTH → used in Cushing's disease.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
GI: N/V, diarrhea, cramping (inject at mealtime to reduce)
Cholelithiasis (impairs gallbladder contraction — US every 6 mo)
Hyperglycemia (blocks insulin secretion; severe with pasireotide)
Bradycardia (mild)
Injection site reactions (depot formulations)
🎯 Clinical Uses
Acromegaly (post-op residual)Cushing's disease (pasireotide)Carcinoid syndromeVIPoma/glucagonomaEsophageal variceal bleeding

Octreotide/lanreotide: suppress GH 50–70% in acromegaly. Monthly LAR depot injections for compliance.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Pasireotide → significant hyperglycemia — blocks insulin secretion (SSTR2 on beta cells). Monitor glucose closely in all patients.
Cholelithiasis: gallstones form because gallbladder emptying is impaired → monitor with US.
Octreotide for esophageal variceal bleeding — reduces portal pressure (↓ splanchnic vasodilation).
Pasireotide hyperglycemia is more severe than octreotide/lanreotide — nearly all patients require glucose-lowering agents. This is due to SSTR2 blockade on pancreatic beta cells (insulin suppression) + SSTR5 effects.
Pegvisomant · Somatropin (rGH) GH Receptor Antagonist · Recombinant GH SC daily · Monitor IGF-1 (not GH) for pegvisomant
⚙ MOA
Pegvisomant: competitive GH receptor antagonist → blocks GH signaling → ↓ IGF-1. GH levels actually RISE (receptor blocked, no feedback removal at pituitary).

Somatropin: recombinant human GH → replaces deficient GH → stimulates IGF-1 production → linear growth, body composition, bone density.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Pegvisomant:
Liver transaminase elevation (monitor LFTs)
Tumor enlargement risk (no direct pituitary effect — no SS analog co-suppression)

Somatropin:
Glucose intolerance (GH is diabetogenic)
Edema, arthralgias
Contraindicated in active malignancy
🎯 Clinical Uses
Pegvisomant: acromegaly resistant to SS analogs
Somatropin: GH deficiency (children + adults)Turner syndromePrader-Willi

Somatropin in adults: improves body composition, ↓ LDL, ↑ bone density. Given SC daily; monitor IGF-1.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Monitor IGF-1 (not GH) for pegvisomant treatment success — GH rises (blocked at receptor, no feedback).
Acromegaly treatment hierarchy: Surgery → SS analogs (octreotide/lanreotide) → dopamine agonists (cabergoline) → pegvisomant.
Somatropin is diabetogenic — GH causes insulin resistance. Monitor glucose.
Pegvisomant raises GH levels — do NOT use GH as treatment monitoring. Use IGF-1 only. Also: pegvisomant has no direct effect on the pituitary tumor — combine with SS analog to prevent tumor growth.

Posterior Pituitary — DDAVP & Diabetes Insipidus

DDAVP (Desmopressin) · Oxytocin Synthetic AVP Analog · V2-Selective Intranasal / PO / IV · Sherman lecture
⚙ MOA
DDAVP: V2-selective synthetic AVP analog → binds V2R on collecting duct → ↑ AQP2 insertion → ↑ water reabsorption → concentrated urine.

Why V2-selective? D-arginine substitution eliminates V1a vasoconstriction. Deaminated N-terminus → resistant to vasopressinase (works in gestational DI).

Oxytocin: binds oxytocin receptor on uterus + breast → uterine contraction + milk letdown.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
DDAVP:
Hyponatremia / water intoxication (excess water retention — dilutional)
Headache, nausea

Oxytocin (Pitocin):
Water intoxication (cross-reacts with V2R at high doses → hyponatremia)
Hypotension (at high IV doses)
Uterine hyperstimulation (fetal distress)
🎯 Clinical Uses
Central DI — 1st lineGestational DIHemophilia A & vWD (DDAVP)
Nocturnal enuresis
Oxytocin: labor induction / postpartum hemorrhage

DDAVP in hemophilia: V2R on endothelium → releases stored vWF + Factor VIII.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
DDAVP works in central DI, fails in nephrogenic DI (V2R or AQP2 defect). Key diagnostic test.
Nephrogenic DI treatment: thiazide diuretics (paradoxical ↓ urine output — volume depletion → ↑ proximal reabsorption). NSAIDs (indomethacin) for lithium-induced nephrogenic DI.
DDAVP for hemophilia A / vWD — releases endothelial stores of vWF and Factor VIII (V2R on endothelial cells).
Thiazide in nephrogenic DI is paradoxical — a diuretic that reduces urine output. Mechanism: volume depletion → ↑ proximal tubule reabsorption → less fluid reaches AVP-sensitive collecting duct → ↓ polyuria. Classic Step 1 paradox.

Thyroid — Hypothyroidism & Hyperthyroidism Drugs

Levothyroxine (T4) · Liothyronine (T3) Thyroid Hormone Replacement PO · Take fasting AM · Monitor TSH 6–8 weeks after dose change
⚙ MOA
Levothyroxine (T4): synthetic T4 prohormone → converted to active T3 by peripheral 5'-deiodinase. T3 binds thyroid receptor (TR) → nuclear gene activation → metabolic effects.

Liothyronine (T3): active form, short t½ (1.5 days vs. T4's 6.5 days) → rarely used clinically due to rapid fluctuations and cardiac risk.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Iatrogenic hyperthyroidism if dose too high: palpitations, weight loss, heat intolerance, atrial fibrillation
Osteoporosis (chronic over-replacement)
Drug interactions: calcium, iron, PPIs, cholestyramine → ↓ absorption (take 4h apart)
Enzyme inducers (rifampin, phenytoin) → ↑ T4 metabolism → need higher dose
🎯 Clinical Uses
Hypothyroidism — 1st linePost-thyroidectomy replacementTSH suppression in thyroid cancer

Post-thyroidectomy for cancer: use TSH-suppressive doses (keep TSH <0.1) to remove trophic stimulus from residual cancer cells.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
T4 is a prohormone; T3 is 10× more potent at thyroid receptor. Peripheral conversion by 5'-deiodinase.
Monitor TSH (not T4 level) to assess adequacy of replacement — goal TSH 0.5–2.5 mIU/L.
Take on empty stomach AM — calcium, iron, and PPIs dramatically ↓ absorption.
Do NOT confuse TSH-suppressive doses (cancer surveillance) with replacement doses. Exogenous T4 → feedback suppresses endogenous TSH — a deliberately suppressed TSH in thyroid cancer post-thyroidectomy is therapeutic, not iatrogenic.
PTU · Methimazole Thionamides — Antithyroid Agents (TPO Inhibitors) PO · PTU: TID (short t½) · Methimazole: once daily
⚙ MOA
Both: block thyroperoxidase (TPO) → inhibit iodide oxidation, organification, and coupling of MIT/DIT → ↓ thyroid hormone synthesis. NOT immediate — existing TH stores must deplete (weeks).

PTU unique: also blocks peripheral 5'-deiodinase → ↓ T4→T3 conversion → faster reduction of active T3 → preferred in thyroid storm.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Agranulocytosis (~0.3–0.5%) — most dangerous; any fever/sore throat → STOP, check CBC immediately
Rash, pruritus, arthralgias (dose-related, both)
PTU: hepatotoxicity — black box warning (severe liver necrosis, rare)
Methimazole: embryopathy (aplasia cutis, choanal atresia, VACTERL) in 1st trimester
🎯 Clinical Uses
Hyperthyroidism (Graves', toxic nodule)Thyroid storm (PTU preferred)Pre-op preparation

Pregnancy rule:
1st trimester → PTU (methimazole teratogenic)
2nd/3rd trimester → methimazole (PTU hepatotoxicity risk)
Breastfeeding → PTU preferred
⭐ High-Yield Facts
PTU for thyroid storm — also blocks T4→T3 conversion (fastest clinical effect on active hormone).
Pregnancy: PTU 1st trimester, methimazole 2nd/3rd trimester. Switch at ~12–14 weeks to ↓ hepatotoxicity risk.
Agranulocytosis: fever + sore throat on thionamide → STOP drug + CBC → medical emergency.
Both take weeks to work — existing TH stores must be depleted. Add beta-blocker for immediate symptom control.
Methimazole in 1st trimester → aplasia cutis (scalp defect), choanal atresia, VACTERL — absolute contraindication. PTU: black box hepatotoxicity — switch to methimazole in 2nd trimester. I-131 is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy (ablates fetal thyroid).
Radioiodine I-131 · Lugol's Iodine · Beta-Blockers Ablative Therapy · Iodine Loading · Symptom Control I-131: PO single dose · Lugol's: pre-op · BB: PO or IV
⚙ MOA
I-131: concentrated by thyroid via NIS → β-radiation → destroys follicular cells. Definitive.

Lugol's (high-dose iodine): Wolff-Chaikoff effect → transiently blocks organification + ↓ thyroid vascularity. Used pre-thyroidectomy (7–10 days) to ↓ intraoperative bleeding.

Beta-blockers (propranolol preferred): block adrenergic symptoms (palpitations, tremor, sweating) + propranolol also blocks T4→T3 conversion.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
I-131:
Hypothyroidism (expected outcome — most patients require lifelong LT4)
Worsening Graves' ophthalmopathy (give steroids)
CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy and breastfeeding

Beta-blockers:
Bronchoconstriction (non-selective — AVOID in asthma)
Bradycardia, hypotension
🎯 Clinical Uses
I-131: Graves' disease (definitive)Toxic nodular goiterLugol's: pre-thyroidectomyBB: immediate symptom control in hyperthyroidism

Beta-blockers: bridge therapy while waiting for thionamides or I-131 to work. Propranolol 40 mg TID is standard.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
I-131 → most patients become hypothyroid and need lifelong levothyroxine. This is the expected outcome, not a complication.
Propranolol blocks T4→T3 conversion (unique among beta-blockers) — fastest way to ↓ active T3 alongside PTU.
Lugol's iodine: paradoxically blocks organification at high doses (Wolff-Chaikoff) — temporary effect, breaks through in 2–3 weeks.
I-131 ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy — crosses placenta and ablates fetal thyroid → permanent neonatal hypothyroidism. Delay conception 6 months after I-131. Graves' ophthalmopathy may worsen with I-131 — pre-treat with steroids.

Adrenal — Testing Agents, Blocking Agents & Replacement (Sharma)

ACTH (Cosyntropin) · CRH · Dexamethasone Adrenal Axis Testing Agents IV/IM (stimulation tests) · PO (dex suppression) · Diagnostic use only
⚙ MOA (Test Logic)
ACTH stimulation (Cosyntropin): give synthetic ACTH → measure cortisol at 30/60 min. Normal: cortisol rises >18–20 µg/dL. Failure → primary adrenal insufficiency (adrenal glands cannot respond).

CRH stimulation: give CRH → measure ACTH + cortisol. Cushing's Disease: ACTH ↑↑ (pituitary responds). Ectopic: ACTH blunted (already maximally secreting, suppressed by negative feedback disrupted).

Dexamethasone suppression: exogenous glucocorticoid → suppresses pituitary ACTH in normal subjects. In Cushing's: insufficient suppression.
⚡ Interpretation Pearls
Low-dose dex (1mg overnight or 2mg/day × 2d):
Normal → cortisol suppresses (<1.8 µg/dL). Abnormal in ANY Cushing's.

High-dose dex (8mg/day × 2d):
Cushing's Disease (pituitary) → cortisol suppresses ≥50% (pituitary still somewhat responsive to feedback).
Ectopic ACTH / adrenal tumor → NO suppression (autonomous).

ACTH level: Primary adrenal insufficiency → ACTH ↑↑ (no cortisol feedback). Secondary → ACTH ↓.
🎯 Clinical Uses
ACTH stim: Addison's diagnosisCRH stim: Cushing's source localizationDex: Cushing's screening + source

Dexamethasone: also used for adrenal crisis prevention (stress dosing), fetal lung maturity (betamethasone/dexamethasone), inflammation.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Low-dose dex: screening test for Cushing's. High-dose dex: localizes source (pituitary vs ectopic vs adrenal).
ACTH level distinguishes primary (↑ ACTH) vs secondary/tertiary (↓ ACTH) adrenal insufficiency.
Cushing's Disease (pituitary): ACTH suppresses >50% on high-dose dex. Ectopic: no suppression at any dose.
Dexamethasone does NOT cross-react with cortisol assays — use dex for suppression tests (not hydrocortisone or prednisone). Pseudo-Cushing's (depression, alcoholism) can show abnormal low-dose dex — use CRH stimulation test to differentiate.
Ketoconazole · Metyrapone · Mitotane · Osilodrostat · Mifepristone · Spironolactone · Eplerenone Adrenal Blocking Agents — Steroid Synthesis Inhibitors / Receptor Blockers PO · Varying monitoring requirements
⚙ MOA
Ketoconazole: inhibits CYP11A1, CYP11B1, 17α-hydroxylase → ↓ cortisol + androgen synthesis (broad steroidogenesis block).

Metyrapone: inhibits CYP11B1 (11β-hydroxylase) → blocks last step in cortisol synthesis (↑ 11-deoxycortisol → ↑ ACTH by feedback → useful diagnostic tool).

Mitotane: adrenolytic — destroys adrenal cortex (used in adrenocortical carcinoma).

Osilodrostat: inhibits CYP11B1 (11β-hydroxylase) → ↓ cortisol (newer, Cushing's).

Mifepristone: glucocorticoid receptor blocker (RU-486) → blocks cortisol effects at receptor level.

Spironolactone/Eplerenone: MRA → blocks mineralocorticoid receptor → ↓ aldosterone effects.
⚡ Side Effects
Ketoconazole:
Hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs), decreased libido, gynecomastia (↓ androgens)

Metyrapone:
Hypertension, hypokalemia, edema (↑ 11-deoxycortisol and other mineralocorticoid precursors)

Mitotane:
GI: N/V/D (severe), neurologic toxicity, adrenal insufficiency

Mifepristone:
Hypokalemia, hypertension (mineralocorticoid activity unblocked)
Endometrial thickening (anti-progesterone effects)

Spironolactone:
Hyperkalemia, gynecomastia, impotence (non-selective)
🎯 Clinical Uses
Cushing's syndrome (all except spiro)Adrenal carcinoma (mitotane)Primary hyperaldosteronism (spiro/eplerenone)HFrEF (spiro/eplerenone)

Cushing's surgery is first-line — drugs are adjuncts when surgery fails/contraindicated or for pre-op cortisol control.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Ketoconazole: most commonly used for medical management of Cushing's. Also: anti-fungal, ↓ testosterone (prostate cancer).
Metyrapone test: blocks CYP11B1 → ↑ 11-deoxycortisol → ↑ ACTH (normal pituitary responds to low cortisol). Used to assess pituitary reserve.
Mifepristone: blocks GR at receptor level → cortisol levels RISE (no feedback blockade) — don't use cortisol to monitor. Use glucose/symptoms.
Mitotane → adrenocortical carcinoma only (destroys adrenal cortex irreversibly). All patients need glucocorticoid replacement.
Mifepristone (GR blocker) → cortisol levels actually INCREASE (no negative feedback via GR at pituitary) — monitor clinical response + glucose, NOT serum cortisol. Spironolactone → gynecomastia/impotence → switch to eplerenone (selective MRA, no androgen/progesterone effects).
Hydrocortisone · Fludrocortisone Adrenal Replacement Hormones — Glucocorticoid + Mineralocorticoid PO daily (physiologic) · IV (adrenal crisis) · Fludrocortisone: PO
⚙ MOA
Hydrocortisone: synthetic cortisol → binds glucocorticoid receptor (GR) → gene activation → anti-inflammatory, glucose regulation, maintain BP, suppress immune response.

Physiologic dose: 15–20 mg/day (divided). Stress dose (illness/surgery): 2–3× physiologic dose.

Fludrocortisone: potent synthetic mineralocorticoid → binds MR → ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption + ↓ K⁺ excretion in collecting duct → maintains BP and electrolytes.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Glucocorticoid excess (supraphysiologic):
Cushing's syndrome features: buffalo hump, moon face, striae, HTN, DM
Adrenal suppression (if chronic → HPA axis suppression → NEVER stop abruptly)
Osteoporosis, peptic ulcer, immunosuppression

Fludrocortisone excess:
Hypertension, hypokalemia, edema
🎯 Clinical Uses
Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's) — both drugsSecondary/tertiary AI — hydrocortisone onlyAdrenal crisis — IV hydrocortisone 100mg q8hCAH — chronic replacement

Secondary AI: no mineralocorticoid needed (aldosterone regulated by RAAS, not ACTH).
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Adrenal crisis: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg q8h + IV fluids + glucose + treat precipitating cause (infection most common).
Primary AI (Addison's) → needs BOTH hydrocortisone (GC) + fludrocortisone (MC). Secondary AI → hydrocortisone only (RAAS intact → aldosterone preserved).
Sick-day rule: double/triple hydrocortisone dose during illness, vomiting, or surgery to prevent adrenal crisis.
Never stop chronic steroids abruptly → HPA axis suppression → adrenal crisis. Must taper.
Secondary AI does NOT need fludrocortisone — aldosterone is regulated by angiotensin II (RAAS), not ACTH. Only primary AI requires mineralocorticoid replacement. Classic vignette trap.

Hypoglycemia (most common — rapid-acting if meal delayed)

Insulin antibodies (rare with modern insulins)

DKA pearls: K⁺ appears HIGH initially (acidosis + insulin deficiency), but total body K⁺ is DEPLETED. Replace K⁺ before starting insulin if K⁺ <3.5.
🎯 Clinical Uses
T1DM — required (absolute insulin deficiency)T2DM — add-on or monotherapyDKA — IV regular insulinGestational DM

Basal-bolus regimen: long-acting basal + rapid-acting with each meal = most physiologic regimen.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Only regular insulin can be given IV (used in DKA and hyperkalemia treatment).
DKA K⁺ paradox: serum K⁺ HIGH despite total body depletion → replete K⁺ BEFORE starting insulin if <3.5 (insulin drives K⁺ into cells → fatal arrhythmia).
Glargine/detemir/degludec: DO NOT MIX with other insulins (altered pH/kinetics). Cannot be given IV.
NPH: cloudy suspension (zinc crystal) — mix last if combining with clear insulin. Peak at 4–10h → overnight hypoglycemia risk.
DKA management: Never start insulin if K⁺ <3.5 — will cause fatal hypokalemia. Give KCl IV first, then start insulin drip. Also: HHS vs DKA — HHS has no acidosis, no ketones, but extreme hyperglycemia (>600) and hyperosmolarity. Higher mortality. Treat with aggressive fluids FIRST.

Diabetes — Injectable Non-Insulin Agents (Janicic)

GLP-1 Agonists · Amylin Analogs (Pramlintide) Incretin Mimetics — GLP-1R Agonists · Amylin Analog SC injection · GLP-1: weekly (semaglutide) or daily (liraglutide) · Pramlintide: with each meal
⚙ MOA
GLP-1 agonists (exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide):
Bind GLP-1R → glucose-dependent insulin secretion (only when glucose elevated) → ↓ glucagon → ↓ gastric emptying → central satiety (hypothalamus) → weight loss.

Pramlintide (amylin analog): synthetic amylin → ↓ glucagon, ↓ gastric emptying, ↑ satiety. Given WITH insulin at meals. Amylin is normally co-secreted with insulin from beta cells.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
GLP-1 agonists:
N/V/D (most common, worst at initiation — improves over weeks)
Pancreatitis (rare, controversial — discontinue if occurs)
Thyroid C-cell tumors (rodent models — black box for liraglutide/semaglutide)
Contraindicated: personal/family Hx MTC, MEN2

Pramlintide:
Hypoglycemia when given with insulin (severe — reduce insulin dose)
N/V
🎯 Clinical Uses
T2DM + ASCVD → GLP-1 agonistT2DM + obesity → semaglutideT1DM adjunct (pramlintide)

CV outcomes: liraglutide (LEADER), semaglutide (SUSTAIN-6) → ↓ MACE in T2DM + established ASCVD. Semaglutide (Wegovy) FDA-approved for obesity.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Glucose-DEPENDENT insulin secretion → NO hypoglycemia when used alone (only hypoglycemic when combined with insulin/sulfonylurea).
GLP-1 agonists: weight loss 3–15 kg via gastric emptying + hypothalamic satiety. First-line add-on in T2DM + ASCVD.
Semaglutide oral (Rybelsus) = first oral GLP-1 agonist. Semaglutide SC (Ozempic) weekly. Wegovy = high-dose semaglutide for obesity.
GLP-1 agonists ≠ DPP-4 inhibitors: GLP-1 agonists are injectable (mostly), 10× more potent, cause significant weight loss. DPP-4 inhibitors: oral, weight neutral, modest glucose lowering. Thyroid C-cell tumor risk: human relevance unclear but black box warning — screen history before prescribing.

Diabetes — Oral Agents (Janicic)

Metformin · Sulfonylureas (Glipizide, Glyburide) Biguanide — AMPK Activator · Sulfonylurea — K-ATP Channel Blocker PO · Metformin: with food (GI SE) · SU: before meals
⚙ MOA
Metformin: activates AMPK → ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis (primary) + ↓ intestinal glucose absorption + ↑ peripheral insulin sensitivity. Does NOT stimulate insulin secretion → no hypoglycemia solo.

Sulfonylureas: bind SUR1 on pancreatic beta cells → close K-ATP channels → membrane depolarization → Ca²⁺ influx → insulin granule exocytosis. Glucose-independent → insulin secreted even in fasting → hypoglycemia risk.
⚡ Side Effects & Contraindications
Metformin:
GI: N/V/D, metallic taste (take with food)
Lactic acidosis (rare — hold with iodinated contrast, surgery; avoid if eGFR <30)
Vitamin B12 deficiency (chronic use)
Contraindicated: eGFR <30, severe hepatic disease, acute illness

Sulfonylureas:
HYPOGLYCEMIA (most important — glucose-independent insulin release)
Weight gain
Glyburide: active metabolites accumulate in renal failure → avoid in CKD/elderly
🎯 Clinical Uses
Metformin: T2DM — 1st line (UKPDS)Pre-diabetes + high-risk (lifestyle failure)
SU: T2DM add-on (cheap, effective)

Metformin: UKPDS trial → ↓ CV events in T2DM. No hypoglycemia, weight neutral/modest loss, cheap, proven.
⭐ High-Yield Facts
Metformin = first-line T2DM. No hypoglycemia, mild weight loss, CV benefit, cheap.
Hold metformin 48h before iodinated contrast (↑ lactic acidosis risk) and resume after confirming normal creatinine.
Sulfonylureas: close K-ATP channels regardless of glucose level → hypoglycemia in fasting. Glyburide worst in CKD (active metabolites).
Lactic acidosis from metformin: rare but serious — eGFR <30, liver failure, alcohol use, contrast agents → risk.
Sulfonylurea mechanism: K-ATP channel closure on beta cells → insulin release even without glucose → hypoglycemia. GLP-1 agonists: same incretin pathway but GLUCOSE-DEPENDENT → no solo hypoglycemia. Key distinction for boards.
SGLT2 Inhibitors · DPP-4 Inhibitors · TZDs · Alpha-Glucosidase Inhibitors Newer Oral Antidiabetics — Multiple Classes PO · SGLT2i: once daily · DPP-4i: once daily · TZD: once daily · AGI: with each meal
⚙ MOA
SGLT2i (canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin): block SGLT2 in PCT → glucosuria → osmotic diuresis → ↓ glucose + ↓ preload (HF benefit). Also anti-fibrotic cardiac/renal effects independent of glucose.

DPP-4i (sitagliptin): inhibit DPP-4 enzyme → ↓ GLP-1 breakdown → ↑ endogenous GLP-1 → glucose-dependent insulin ↑. Less potent than GLP-1 agonists.

TZD (pioglitazone): activate PPARγ → ↑ adipogenesis + ↑ peripheral insulin sensitivity (fat and muscle). Slow onset (6–12 wks).

Acarbose (α-glucosidase inhibitor): blocks intestinal α-glucosidase → delays CHO absorption → ↓ postprandial glucose spike.
⚡ Side Effects
SGLT2i: genital mycotic infections, UTI, euglycemic DKA, hypotension
Avoid if eGFR <20; Type 1 DM (↑ DKA)

DPP-4i: generally well tolerated; pancreatitis (rare); saxagliptin → ↑ HF hospitalizations (avoid in HF)

TZD (pioglitazone):
Weight gain + fluid retention → edema → can worsen HF
Bladder cancer risk (pioglitazone — avoid if Hx bladder cancer)
Fractures (↓ bone density in women)
AVOID in HFrEF

Acarbose: GI bloating, diarrhea, flatulence (CHO fermentation)
🎯 Clinical Uses
SGLT2i: T2DM + HFrEF or HFpEFT2DM + CKD (canagliflozin/dapagliflozin)
DPP-4i: T2DM (weight-neutral option)
TZD: T2DM + insulin resistance (no HF)
Acarbose: postprandial hyperglycemia, dumping syndrome
⭐ High-Yield Facts
SGLT2i: ONLY class with benefit in BOTH HFrEF AND HFpEF (EMPEROR-Reduced, EMPEROR-Preserved, DAPA-HF). Works regardless of DM.
Euglycemic DKA: SGLT2i → DKA with near-normal glucose. Always check ketones, not just glucose.
TZDs: AVOID in HFrEF (fluid retention worsens HF). Pioglitazone: check for bladder cancer history.
DPP-4i: weight-neutral, no hypoglycemia, oral. Saxagliptin: ↑ HF hospitalizations → avoid in HF.
SGLT2i in a non-diabetic with HFrEF → still indicated (mechanism beyond glucose). Euglycemic DKA — glucose 180 mg/dL doesn't rule out DKA when on SGLT2i. TZD + HFrEF = dangerous fluid retention → absolutely contraindicated.
