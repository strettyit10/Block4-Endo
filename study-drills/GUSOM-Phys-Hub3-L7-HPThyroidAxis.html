Q1
A 32-year-old woman presents with 3 months of palpitations, heat intolerance, 12-pound weight loss, and insomnia. Exam shows exophthalmos, lid lag, and a diffusely enlarged thyroid. TSH is undetectable, free T4 is 3× normal. Thyroid uptake scan shows diffuse increased uptake. What is the first-line pharmacological treatment in the non-pregnant patient?
A) Propranolol alone
B) Propylthiouracil (PTU)
C) Methimazole (MMI)
D) Radioiodine (RAI) ablation
E) Thyroidectomy
Explanation
Graves disease: diffuse toxic goiter, TSI positive, increased RAI uptake. First-line pharmacological: methimazole (MMI) — preferred over PTU in non-pregnant adults (once-daily dosing, better safety profile). PTU is used in: first trimester of pregnancy, thyroid storm (faster T4→T3 conversion blockade), methimazole allergy. MMI mechanism: inhibits thyroid peroxidase (TPO) → blocks iodide organification and coupling → ↓T4/T3 synthesis. Does NOT block release of stored hormone (so symptom control takes weeks — use propranolol for symptomatic relief early).
MMI = first-line for Graves (non-pregnant). PTU = pregnancy (first trimester), thyroid storm. Both block TPO. Neither blocks stored hormone release — symptoms take weeks to improve.
Q2
A 45-year-old man with a 3 cm thyroid nodule undergoes FNA. Pathology shows cells with 'orphan Annie eye' nuclei, nuclear grooves, and psammoma bodies. What is the diagnosis, and what molecular marker is most commonly associated?
A) Follicular thyroid cancer — RAS mutation
B) Medullary thyroid cancer — RET point mutation
C) Anaplastic thyroid cancer — p53 mutation
D) Papillary thyroid cancer — BRAF V600E mutation
E) Hurthle cell carcinoma — mitochondrial mutations
Explanation
Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) — most common thyroid cancer (80%). Histologic hallmarks: (1) Orphan Annie eye nuclei (empty-appearing ground glass nuclei with nuclear clearing), (2) nuclear grooves, (3) intranuclear cytoplasmic inclusions, (4) psammoma bodies (concentric calcifications). Molecular: BRAF V600E most common (~60% of PTC), RET/PTC rearrangements (especially post-radiation). Prognosis: excellent (10-year survival >90%). Treatment: thyroidectomy ± RAI ± TSH suppression.
PTC: Orphan Annie nuclei + psammoma bodies + nuclear grooves. BRAF V600E (most common). RET/PTC (radiation-associated). Best prognosis. BRAF = also in melanoma and colon cancer.
Q3
A 25-year-old woman delivers a healthy baby. 3 months postpartum she develops palpitations and irritability, then at 6 months she develops fatigue and cold intolerance. TSH is now elevated with low T4. Anti-TPO antibodies are positive. RAI uptake was low during the initial hyperthyroid phase. What is the diagnosis?
A) Graves disease developing postpartum
B) Subacute (de Quervain) thyroiditis
C) Postpartum thyroiditis
D) Toxic multinodular goiter
E) TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma
Explanation
Postpartum thyroiditis: autoimmune destructive thyroiditis occurring within 1 year postpartum. Pattern: hyperthyroid phase (1-4 months, follicle destruction → T4/T3 release, low RAI uptake) → hypothyroid phase (4-8 months, depleted stores) → recovery (most). Anti-TPO positive. Distinguished from Graves: low RAI uptake during hyper phase (Graves has high uptake) + no TSI. 20-30% progress to permanent hypothyroidism. Treatment: β-blocker for hyper phase (symptomatic), levothyroxine for hypo phase if symptomatic.
Postpartum thyroiditis: hyper → hypo → usually recovery. LOW RAI uptake (destructive, not synthetic). Anti-TPO positive. Distinguish from Graves (high RAI, TSI positive).
Q4
A 55-year-old man on amiodarone for 2 years develops hypothyroidism. TSH is 12.4, free T4 is low. What are the THREE mechanisms by which amiodarone causes hypothyroidism?
A) Direct thyrocyte apoptosis only
B) Wolff-Chaikoff effect (excess iodine inhibits organification) + inhibition of 5'-deiodinase (↓T4→T3) + direct toxic thyroiditis
C) Inhibition of TSH receptors
D) Anti-TPO antibody induction
E) Block of NIS (sodium-iodide symporter) only
Explanation
Amiodarone (40% iodine by weight) causes hypothyroidism via: (1) Wolff-Chaikoff effect: excess iodine → blocks organification → ↓T4/T3 synthesis. Normal thyroids escape; those with underlying autoimmune disease cannot escape → hypothyroidism. (2) Inhibition of 5'-deiodinase: ↓peripheral T4→T3 conversion (this elevates T4 and rT3, lowers T3 — this is expected and not disease). (3) Direct toxic thyroiditis (type 2 amiodarone hyperthyroidism mechanism when it damages cells → releases T4/T3). Treatment: levothyroxine (continue amiodarone if needed for arrhythmia).
Amiodarone hypo: Wolff-Chaikoff + ↓5'-deiodinase + direct thyroiditis. Expected labs on amiodarone: ↑T4, ↓T3, ↑rT3, ↑TSH slightly initially. Treat hypo with levothyroxine.
Q5
A 68-year-old man has a thyroid nodule with FNA showing 'vascular invasion.' Histology also shows follicular architecture without the diagnostic nuclear features of papillary cancer. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Papillary thyroid cancer
B) Follicular thyroid adenoma (benign)
C) Follicular thyroid carcinoma
D) Medullary thyroid cancer
E) Hashimoto thyroiditis with reactive nodule
Explanation
Follicular thyroid carcinoma: distinguished from follicular adenoma ONLY by capsular and/or vascular invasion on histology (cannot be determined by FNA cytology alone — FNA shows follicular cells but cannot assess invasion). Molecular: RAS mutations, PAX8-PPARγ rearrangement. Prognosis: good if minimally invasive; worse if widely invasive (hematogenous spread — lung, bone). FNA result 'follicular neoplasm/suspicious' → requires surgery for definitive diagnosis.
Follicular carcinoma vs adenoma: requires CAPSULAR and/or VASCULAR INVASION on histology. FNA cannot distinguish — requires surgical specimen. Spreads hematogenously (unlike papillary, which spreads lymphatically).
Q6
A 48-year-old woman is found to have TSH of 8.2 mIU/L on routine screening. Free T4 is normal. She has no symptoms. Anti-TPO antibodies are positive. What is the diagnosis and management?
A) Overt hypothyroidism — start levothyroxine immediately
B) Subclinical hypothyroidism — consider treatment if TSH >10, symptomatic, or pregnant
C) Hashimoto thyroiditis with normal function — no treatment, follow-up only
D) SIADH from TSH elevation
E) Subclinical hyperthyroidism — suppress TSH further
Explanation
Subclinical hypothyroidism: elevated TSH (4.5-10 mIU/L) + NORMAL free T4. Treatment is indicated if: TSH >10 mIU/L, symptoms present, pregnancy, infertility, significant lipid abnormalities. With TSH 8.2 and no symptoms: generally observe (recheck in 6 months). Anti-TPO positive = higher risk of progression to overt hypothyroidism (~5%/year). The goal of levothyroxine: normalize TSH to 0.5-2.5 mIU/L.
Subclinical hypo: ↑TSH + normal T4. Treat if TSH >10, symptomatic, pregnant, or infertile. Anti-TPO positive = higher progression risk. Annual TSH monitoring.
Q7
Radioiodine (RAI, I-131) therapy is used to treat Graves disease. Which patients should receive pre-treatment glucocorticoids before RAI?
A) All Graves patients before RAI
B) Only patients with active severe Graves ophthalmopathy — RAI can worsen eye disease
C) Patients with elevated anti-TPO antibodies
D) Patients with TSH <0.01
E) All patients with macroadenoma compressing the optic chiasm
Explanation
RAI and ophthalmopathy: RAI treatment can worsen or precipitate Graves ophthalmopathy in susceptible patients, particularly smokers and those with moderate-severe active ophthalmopathy. Mechanism: thyroid cell destruction → release of TSH receptor antigens → increased autoimmune response against orbital tissue. Management: patients with moderate-severe active ophthalmopathy should receive prophylactic oral glucocorticoids concurrent with RAI. Mild/inactive ophthalmopathy: can receive RAI without steroids. Smoking is the major modifiable risk factor for worsening ophthalmopathy.
RAI + active severe ophthalmopathy = risk of worsening. Give concurrent steroids. Mild/inactive ophthalmopathy: RAI without steroids is acceptable. Smoking = major risk factor.
Q8
A 38-year-old woman presents to the ER with fever (40°C), tachycardia (140 bpm), severe agitation, and vomiting. She recently had an upper respiratory infection. TSH is undetectable, T4 is 5× normal. What is the immediate treatment?
A) Methimazole only, then observe
B) Propranolol + PTU + glucocorticoids + potassium iodide (Lugol's) in sequence
C) Emergency thyroidectomy
D) Radioiodine to quickly reduce thyroid hormone
E) Plasmapheresis alone
Explanation
Thyroid storm: life-threatening emergency. Treatment targets multiple steps simultaneously: (1) PTU first (blocks synthesis AND T4→T3 conversion — PREFERRED over MMI in storm). (2) 1-2 hours AFTER PTU: Lugol's iodine (Wolff-Chaikoff — blocks release of stored hormone; must give PTU first or iodine provides substrate → more hormone). (3) Propranolol (↓sympathetic symptoms, ↓T4→T3 peripherally). (4) Glucocorticoids (dexamethasone — ↓T4→T3 conversion + possible adrenal insufficiency coverage). (5) Treat precipitating cause.
Thyroid storm: PTU first → then iodide (1-2h later) → propranolol → glucocorticoids. PTU preferred over MMI (also blocks T4→T3). Never give iodide before PTU (would stimulate more synthesis).
Q9
Hashimoto thyroiditis is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in the developed world. What immune cells are primarily responsible for thyroid destruction?
A) B cells producing anti-TPO antibodies that directly lyse thyrocytes
B) CD4+ TH2 cells stimulating antibody production
C) CD8+ cytotoxic T cells directly attacking thyrocytes + CD4+ TH1 cells orchestrating the response
D) Natural killer cells
E) Mast cells and eosinophils
Explanation
Hashimoto thyroiditis: TH1-predominant autoimmune response. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells directly kill thyrocytes (perforin/granzyme + Fas-FasL pathway). CD4+ TH1 helper cells secrete IFN-γ → activate macrophages + cytotoxic T cells. Germinal center formation within thyroid → autoantibody production (anti-TPO most sensitive >95%, anti-TgAb). Hurthle cells (eosinophilic metaplasia of follicular cells) = characteristic histology. End result: thyroid lymphocytic infiltration, fibrosis, hypothyroidism.
Hashimoto: TH1 + CD8+ T cells → thyrocyte destruction. Anti-TPO >95% sensitive (not specific — also in Graves). Hurthle cells on histology. Lymphocytic infiltration + germinal centers.
Q10
A 40-year-old woman presents with a painless anterior neck mass, 3 weeks after a URI. She denies thyroid pain. TSH is normal. Ultrasound shows a 1.5 cm cold nodule. What is the most important next step?
A) RAI uptake scan
B) CT neck with contrast
C) Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy
D) Repeat TSH in 6 months
E) Measure anti-TPO and anti-TgAb
Explanation
Thyroid nodule management: FNA biopsy is the most important next step for a cold nodule (low/no RAI uptake) on workup — cold nodules have ~10-15% malignancy rate. Algorithm: TSH normal or high → ultrasound → if nodule ≥1 cm with concerning features OR ≥1.5 cm → FNA. TSH suppressed → RAI scan first (hot nodule = toxic adenoma, less likely malignant). Key: cold nodule = cannot rule out malignancy → FNA is definitive.
Cold nodule + normal/high TSH → FNA. Hot nodule + low TSH → RAI scan (likely toxic adenoma = benign). FNA is the gold standard for nodule evaluation.
BRS Q1
TSH stimulates thyroid follicular cells through which receptor mechanism?
A) Tyrosine kinase receptor → JAK-STAT
B) Nuclear receptor → direct DNA binding
C) Gs-coupled GPCR → ↑adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA → thyroid hormone synthesis and secretion
D) Gi-coupled → ↓cAMP
E) Gq → PLC → IP3 → Ca2+ → thyroid hormone release
Explanation
TSH receptor (TSHR) → Gs-coupled GPCR → ↑adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA → multiple effects: (1) ↑NIS expression (iodide uptake), (2) ↑thyroglobulin synthesis, (3) ↑TPO activity (organification), (4) ↑iodothyronine deiodinase, (5) thyroid cell growth (goiter). TSI (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin in Graves) acts through the same TSHR → Gs → cAMP pathway. GNAS activating mutations can also autonomously activate thyroid cell cAMP → toxic adenoma (Gs pathway is the key).
TSHR → Gs → cAMP → PKA → all aspects of T4/T3 synthesis. TSI acts at same receptor. Autonomous toxic adenoma = constitutive TSHR activation → low TSH (feedback suppressed).
BRS Q2
A patient in the ICU develops the 'sick euthyroid syndrome.' Which laboratory pattern is most characteristic?
A) Low T3, low T4, elevated TSH
B) High T3, normal T4, low TSH
C) Low T3, normal-low T4, low-normal TSH
D) Normal T3, high T4, high TSH
E) Low T3, high T4, high TSH
Explanation
Sick euthyroid (non-thyroidal illness): (1) T3 FALLS (↓5'-deiodinase activity → less T4→T3 conversion; also ↑reverse T3). (2) T4 may be normal early, then falls in severe illness (↓thyroid-binding globulin, ↑clearance). (3) TSH is low or low-normal (cytokines suppress hypothalamic TRH → ↓TSH). Pattern: Low T3, normal-to-low T4, low-normal TSH. This is the most common pattern in ICU. Treatment: none. rT3 is elevated (T4 → rT3 instead of T3).
Sick euthyroid: ↓T3 (key), ↑rT3, ±↓T4, low-normal TSH. Do not treat — adaptive response. Recheck TFTs 6-8 weeks after recovery.
BRS Q3
Which of the following is the primary active thyroid hormone at the cellular level, and how does it exert its effects?
A) T4 — binds to cytoplasmic receptor
B) T3 — binds to nuclear thyroid hormone receptor (TR), regulates gene transcription
C) Reverse T3 — binds nuclear TR with highest affinity
D) MIT (monoiodotyrosine) — directly activates thyroid hormone receptor
E) T4 directly activates membrane receptors
Explanation
T3 is the primary active thyroid hormone (3-4× more potent than T4, higher TR affinity). T4 is a prohormone, converted peripherally by 5'-deiodinase (type 1 in liver/kidney, type 2 in brain/pituitary) to T3. T3 → binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptor (TR-α, TR-β) → TH response element (TRE) on DNA → regulates gene transcription. Effects: ↑basal metabolic rate, ↑O2 consumption, ↑thermogenesis, ↑sympathetic sensitivity (↑β-adrenergic receptors), heart, brain development (critical in fetus).
T4 = prohormone. T3 = active hormone. T3 → nuclear TR → TRE → gene transcription. 5'-deiodinase converts T4→T3. rT3 is inactive (competitive inhibitor of deiodinase).
BRS Q4
The Wolff-Chaikoff effect refers to which phenomenon?
A) High TSH stimulates iodide uptake
B) Acute iodine excess → transient inhibition of thyroid hormone organification
C) Chronic iodine deficiency → goiter
D) Excess iodine permanently inhibits the NIS symporter
E) Low iodine activates thyroid peroxidase
Explanation
Wolff-Chaikoff effect: acute high iodine load (e.g., contrast, Lugol's, amiodarone) → iodide accumulates in thyroid → inhibits thyroglobulin organification (iodide incorporation into tyrosyl residues) → transient ↓T4/T3 synthesis. Normal thyroid escapes within 1-2 weeks (↓NIS expression → less iodide uptake). Clinical uses: Lugol's iodine pre-thyroidectomy (reduces vascularity) and acute thyroid storm (after PTU administration). Patients with autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto) cannot escape → may develop permanent hypothyroidism.
Wolff-Chaikoff: high iodine → ↓organification → transient ↓T4/T3. Normal escape in 1-2 weeks. Autoimmune thyroid disease = cannot escape → hypothyroidism. Lugol's used in thyroid surgery prep.
BRS Q5
A patient is found to have an elevated TSH of 18 mIU/L with low free T4. She is started on levothyroxine. TSH normalizes to 1.8 mIU/L after 8 weeks. Why is TSH used to monitor levothyroxine therapy in primary hypothyroidism?
A) TSH directly stimulates T4 synthesis in the thyroid
B) TSH reflects the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3
C) The pituitary TSH-secreting cells are exquisitely sensitive to free T4 — TSH is the most sensitive indicator of overall thyroid hormone status
D) TSH levels predict free T4 more accurately than direct T4 measurement
E) TSH monitoring is only used in central hypothyroidism
Explanation
In PRIMARY hypothyroidism (pituitary is intact), TSH is the gold standard monitoring tool because: (1) Thyrotrophs are exquisitely sensitive to free T4 — tiny changes in T4 cause large changes in TSH (log-linear relationship). (2) TSH reflects the INTEGRATED T4/T3 action over days-weeks (more stable than single T4 measurements). (3) TSH normalizes within 6-8 weeks of adequate T4 replacement. Exception: central (secondary) hypothyroidism → TSH is low or inappropriately normal → must monitor free T4 instead.
Primary hypothyroidism: monitor TSH (intact pituitary, exquisite sensitivity). Central hypothyroidism: monitor free T4 (pituitary cannot generate appropriate TSH). This is a high-yield distinction.
