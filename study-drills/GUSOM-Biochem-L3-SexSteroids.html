Biochem Lecture 3: Sex Steroid Synthesis and Action

Flashcard Guide: Click any card to flip and reveal the answer. Each card corresponds to one or more Anki decks for cross-reference.
Q: Anki #10 - Where are Leydig cells located, and what hormone drives their function?
A: Leydig cells (interstitial cells) are located between seminiferous tubules. They respond to LH and produce testosterone. Their strategic position allows testosterone to enter either the seminiferous tubules (crossing the blood-testis barrier into Sertoli cells) or directly into circulation.
Q: Anki #11 - What do Sertoli cells produce?
A: Sertoli cells respond to FSH and produce: (1) Androgen Binding Protein (ABP) - concentrates testosterone locally; (2) Inhibin - selectively suppresses FSH (not LH); (3) Blood-testis barrier - protects developing gametes.
Q: Anki #12 - How is testosterone converted to DHT, and what drug blocks this?
A: 5α-reductase catalyzes testosterone → DHT in skin (genitalia) and prostate. Finasteride is a selective 5α-reductase inhibitor (used for BPH and androgenetic alopecia). DHT is ~5× more potent than testosterone.
Q: Anki #13 - How is testosterone converted to estradiol?
A: Aromatase (CYP19) catalyzes testosterone → 17β-estradiol. Occurs in: granulosa cells (ovary), Sertoli cells (testis), and adipose tissue. Letrozole and Anastrozole block aromatase (breast cancer therapy).
Q: Anki #14 - Explain the two-cell two-gonadotropin model for ovarian estradiol synthesis.
A: Theca cells (LH-responsive): Produce androstenedione and testosterone from cholesterol. Granulosa cells (FSH-responsive): Upregulate aromatase and convert thecal androgens to estradiol. Critical: Neither cell type alone can produce estradiol; cooperation is mandatory.
Q: Anki #16 - What is SHBG and how do hormones regulate it?
A: SHBG (Sex Hormone Binding Globulin) is synthesized in the liver and binds ~60% of circulating testosterone and estradiol. Estrogen ↑ SHBG synthesis; Androgens ↓ SHBG. Only ~2% of testosterone circulates free (biologically active). Obesity decreases SHBG levels.
Q: Anki #17 - How are FSH and LH secreted, and what is their structure?
A: Gonadotrophs (anterior pituitary) secrete both FSH and LH in response to GnRH pulses. Structure: Both are glycoprotein heterodimers with identical α-subunits but distinct β-subunits (giving biological specificity). Distinct feedback inhibition (inhibin suppresses FSH only).
Q: Anki #20 - How does stress cause amenorrhea?
A: GnIH (Gonadotropin-Inhibiting Hormone) suppresses GnRH release during stress, illness, or energy deficit. ↓ GnRH → ↓ FSH/LH → ↓ estradiol → amenorrhea. Seen in eating disorders and endurance athletes. Reversible with stress reduction and weight restoration.
Q: Anki #21 - What is Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (AIS)?
A: 46,XY individuals with nonfunctional androgen receptors. Despite ↑ testosterone, tissues cannot respond. Phenotype: Female external genitalia, blind-ending vagina, NO internal uterus (Müllerian Inhibiting Substance still active), undescended testes, normal to ↑ LH.
Q: Anki #8 - What is the presentation of 5α-reductase deficiency?
A: At birth: Female/ambiguous external genitalia (↓ DHT). At puberty: Dramatic virilization (testosterone accumulates). Classic: "penis at 12." Raised as female → often reassign to male at puberty. No gynecomastia (testosterone preferentially binds AR, not aromatase pathway as dominant).
Q: Anki #9 - What is 17β-HSD deficiency?
A: 17β-HSD catalyzes: androstenedione → testosterone and estrone → estradiol. Deficiency causes ↓ testosterone, ↑ androstenedione. 46,XY: Female external at birth, mild virilization at puberty. 46,XX: Normal female development (estrone/peripheral aromatization compensate).
Q: Anki #193 - How do oral contraceptive pills prevent pregnancy?
A: OCPs (ethinyl estradiol + progestin): (1) ↑ negative feedback → ↓ FSH/LH; (2) ↓ LH → no ovulation, ↓ theca androgen production; (3) ↓ FSH → no follicular growth; (4) Progestin thickens cervical mucus, alters endometrium. ~0.3% failure rate (perfect use), ~9% (typical use).
Q: Anki #194 - What triggers fetal testicular testosterone production?
A: 1st trimester: hCG (from placental syncytiotrophoblast) stimulates fetal Leydig cells → testosterone. 2nd-3rd trimester: Fetal pituitary FSH/LH take over. Critical window (weeks 8-14): testosterone/DHT masculinize external genitalia; MIS suppresses Müllerian ducts.
Q: Anki #195 - What is the feedback action of inhibin?
A: Inhibin is produced by Sertoli cells (FSH-stimulated). Selectively suppresses FSH only (not LH) at the pituitary and hypothalamus. Clinical pearl: In infertile males with isolated ↑ FSH, suspect Sertoli cell damage with ↓ inhibin production.
Q: Anki #196 - Why is peripheral aromatization important?
A: Aromatase in adipose tissue converts testosterone → estradiol. Postmenopausal: no ovarian estrogen → relies on peripheral aromatization (especially in adipose). Obesity: ↑ aromatase → ↑ estradiol → gynecomastia in men, ↑ breast cancer risk in women. Treatment: Aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole).
Q: Anki #197 - How do SHBG levels change with menopause?
A: Postmenopause: ↓ estradiol → ↓ SHBG synthesis → ↓ total SHBG. Results in ↑ free testosterone. Also ↑ adrenal androstenedione → peripheral aromatization to estrone (becomes dominant estrogen). May develop androgenic features (hirsutism).
Q: Anki #198 - What happens to the HPG axis with anabolic steroid use?
A: Exogenous androgens: (1) ↓ GnRH/FSH/LH via negative feedback; (2) Sertoli atrophy → ↓ inhibin; (3) ↓ spermatogenesis (azoospermia); (4) Gynecomastia (aromatization). Reversible: FSH normalizes in 3-6 months; testicular recovery takes longer (variable).
Q: Anki #199 - What is the hormone pattern in Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)?
A: Klinefelter (47,XXY): Small testes, azoospermia, tall stature. Hormones: ↑ LH (loss of feedback), ↓ testosterone (testicular failure), ↑ FSH (tubular atrophy). May develop gynecomastia (↑ estradiol from aromatization). Diagnosis: Often incidental on infertility evaluation.
Q: Anki #200 - What does isolated FSH elevation suggest in an infertile male?
A: Isolated ↑ FSH (with normal LH): Seminiferous tubule damage (primary tubular failure), not Leydig cell failure. Causes: cryptorchidism, chemotherapy, radiation, infection. Mechanism: Tubular atrophy → ↓ inhibin → ↓ FSH suppression. LH normal (Leydig cells intact, testosterone adequate).
Q: Anki #201 - How does estrogen affect lipid and clotting parameters?
A: Estrogen increases: HDL, triglycerides, fibrinogen, clotting factors (↑ VTE risk on OCPs), SHBG, CBG, angiotensinogen. Decreases: LDL, total cholesterol (modest). Clinical: OCPs can ↑ BP, ↑ VTE, ↓ glucose tolerance. Risk factors (smoking, age >35) contraindicate estrogen use.
Q: Anki #202 - What is the characteristic hormone pattern in PCOS?
A: PCOS: ↑ LH/FSH ratio (↑ LH, low-normal/↓ FSH). Hyperandrogenism: ↑ testosterone, ↑ androstenedione (theca hyperplasia). ↓ FSH → ↓ aromatase → ↓ local estradiol → arrest of follicular development (anovulation). Mechanism: Abnormal GnRH frequency → ↑ relative LH.
Q: Anki #203 - How do FSH and LH regulate the menstrual cycle?
A: Follicular phase: ↑ FSH recruits follicles, upregulates aromatase in granulosa. Mid-cycle surge: LH surge triggers ovulation and androgen production. Luteal phase: Corpus luteum produces progesterone; inhibin/progesterone ↓ FSH/LH. Luteolysis: Endometrial PGF2α → ↓ progesterone → menses.
Q: Anki #204 - How is testosterone metabolized?
A: Hepatic metabolism: 17-ketosteroid reductase → androsterone, etiocholanolone (conjugated as glucuronide/sulfate). Plasma t1/2: 10-20 minutes. Urinary 17-ketosteroids: Reflect total androgen production. Local metabolism: Adipose/skin → DHT or aromatize to estradiol.
Q: Anki #205 - What is Müllerian Inhibiting Substance (MIS)?
A: MIS (Anti-Müllerian Hormone, AMH) produced by Sertoli cells from week 7 gestation. Causes Müllerian duct regression in males. Females: No MIS → Müllerian structures develop. Clinical: ↑ AMH = more ovarian reserve; ↓ AMH = reduced fertility potential.
Q: Anki #206 - How does progesterone exert its effects?
A: Progesterone receptor (PR): Cytoplasmic → nuclear translocation upon hormone binding. Mechanisms: (1) Direct binding to progesterone response elements (PREs); (2) Interaction with other TFs. Tissue effects: Uterus (secretory changes, ↓ contractility), breast (↓ proliferation), brain (↓ GnRH). Progestins used in contraception, HRT, cycle disorders.
Q: Anki #207 - What is the structure and mechanism of androgen receptors?
A: Androgen receptor (AR): Ligand-activated transcription factor. Domains: N-terminal (transactivation), DNA-binding (zinc fingers), hinge, ligand-binding (C-terminal). Mechanism: T/DHT binding → nuclear translocation → binding to androgen response elements (AREs). DHT ~2.4× more potent than testosterone (higher receptor affinity).
Q: Anki #208 - What are estrogen receptor subtypes?
A: ER-α: Ubiquitous; mediates proliferation, bone protection. ER-β: Prostate, colon, bone; often anti-proliferative. GPER: Non-genomic signaling (rapid). Clinical: SERMs (selective ER modulators) like tamoxifen act differently in breast (antagonist) vs. bone (agonist).
Q: Anki #209 - What is the difference between genomic and non-genomic steroid actions?
A: Genomic (slow): Steroid → cytoplasmic/nuclear receptor → nuclear translocation → gene transcription (minutes-hours). Non-genomic (rapid): Steroid → membrane receptor → rapid kinase cascades (seconds-minutes). Examples: Estrogen vasodilation (GPER), testosterone phosphorylation (membrane AR, Src kinase).
Q: Anki #210 - How are aromatase inhibitors used in breast cancer?
A: Aromatase inhibitors (Letrozole, Anastrozole, Exemestane): Block conversion of androgens → estrogens (postmenopausal). Use: ER-positive breast cancer (1st-line postmenopausal). Mechanism: Deprive tumor of estradiol. Side effects: ↓ bone density, joint pain, lipid changes. Non-steroidal AIs preferred over steroidal due to better tolerability.
Q: Anki #211 - What is 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis?
A: 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis: Streak gonads (non-functional). Phenotype: Female external/internal genitalia (no testosterone/MIS). Hormones: ↓ testosterone, ↑ LH, ↑ FSH (primary hypogonadism). Causes: SRY mutations, DHH, NR5A1, or idiopathic. Treatment: Hormone replacement (estrogen + progesterone) for menses and bone health.

Vignette 1: Androgen Insensitivity at Birth
A newborn baby assigned female at birth undergoes prenatal ultrasound for other reasons. MRI shows streak gonads, no internal female structures (no uterus or fallopian tubes), and a blind-ending vaginal pouch. Blood work at age 2 months: testosterone 156 ng/dL (normal for male), LH elevated, normal FSH.
What is the most likely diagnosis?
Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (46,XY AIS). Female external genitalia (no virilization due to AR deficiency), NO uterus (MIS from undescended testes still functional), normal to high testosterone (LH can stimulate Leydig cells, but AR nonfunctional). Genetic testing would confirm AR gene mutation. Management: gonadectomy (↑ malignancy risk in streak gonads), hormone replacement (estrogen/progesterone for bone health and puberty).
Vignette 2: 5α-Reductase Deficiency & Virilization at Puberty
A 13-year-old raised as female presents to endocrinology with sudden onset of facial hair, clitoral enlargement, and voice deepening over the past 6 months. Parents report she has been increasingly distressed and wishes to be referred to as "he." Testosterone 678 ng/dL (elevated), DHT low-normal, normal LH. Karyotype: 46,XY. External genitalia examination reveals clitoromegaly consistent with a micropenis.
What is the diagnosis, and why does virilization occur AT PUBERTY?
5α-Reductase deficiency. Normal testosterone production (LH function intact), but DHT cannot be formed. At birth: ↓ DHT → female/ambiguous genitalia. At puberty: ↑ testosterone accumulates → binds androgen receptor in target tissues (even without DHT conversion) → partial virilization. Classic presentation: "penis at 12." Gender identity management requires sensitive discussion. Long-term hormone management depends on patient preference.
Vignette 3: 17β-HSD Deficiency
A 46,XY newborn with female external genitalia is referred for sex assignment counseling. Labs show very low testosterone, elevated androstenedione, low estradiol, low-normal LH. Genetic testing reveals loss-of-function mutations in the HSD17B3 gene (17β-HSD type 3).
What enzyme is deficient, and what is the biochemical consequence?
17β-HSD type 3 deficiency. This enzyme catalyzes the final step: androstenedione → testosterone (and estrone → estradiol). Deficiency results in ↓ testosterone, ↑ androstenedione. At birth: female external (↓ testosterone/DHT for virilization). At puberty: gradual testosterone accumulation (some conversion via alternate pathways) → mild virilization. 46,XX individuals: Often unaffected or minimally virilized (estrone compensates for ↓ estradiol).
Vignette 4: Obese Man with Gynecomastia
A 58-year-old obese man (BMI 38) with type 2 diabetes presents with bilateral breast enlargement and tenderness. He denies anabolic steroid use, medications that cause gynecomastia, and has no signs of liver disease. Labs: testosterone 380 ng/dL (low-normal), estradiol 42 pg/mL (elevated), LH mildly elevated, normal prolactin.
What is the PRIMARY mechanism of gynecomastia in this patient?
Peripheral aromatization of testosterone → estradiol in adipose tissue. Obesity increases aromatase expression in adipose, converting testosterone to estradiol. Result: ↑ local estradiol → gynecomastia. Secondary: obesity causes ↓ SHBG → ↑ free testosterone → ↑ substrate for aromatase. Management: Weight loss (reduces aromatase activity), aromatase inhibitor if symptomatic, or surgical intervention if severe/persistent.
Vignette 5: Postmenopausal Woman with SHBG & Androgen Excess
A 56-year-old postmenopausal woman (2 years since last menses) presents with new-onset hirsutism, male-pattern baldness, and acne. Labs: total testosterone 65 ng/dL (normal for postmenopausal), FREE testosterone 2.8 pg/mL (elevated), SHBG 18 nmol/L (low), estradiol 12 pg/mL (low). No adrenal or ovarian tumors on imaging.
Why is FREE testosterone elevated despite normal TOTAL testosterone?
↓ Estradiol → ↓ SHBG synthesis → lower total SHBG levels. With lower SHBG, the same amount of testosterone circulates as free (unbound) rather than SHBG-bound. Androgens suppress SHBG, creating a self-perpetuating cycle. This explains androgenic symptoms in postmenopausal women despite "normal" total testosterone levels. Treatment: HRT with estrogen (↑ SHBG) or spironolactone (androgen receptor antagonist).
Vignette 6: Athlete with FSH Suppression & Amenorrhea
A 26-year-old elite marathon runner with 5% body fat presents with secondary amenorrhea (no menses × 14 months). Labs: FSH 1.8 mIU/L (low), LH 1.2 mIU/L (low), estradiol <5 pg/mL (low), free testosterone 0.5 pg/mL (low), normal prolactin and TSH. She denies extreme stress beyond training but admits poor nutrition during heavy training cycles.
What hormone is suppressing GnRH release, and what is the mechanism?
GnIH (Gonadotropin-Inhibiting Hormone) is suppressing GnRH release in response to low energy availability and/or training stress. Low body fat (↓ leptin) and negative energy balance activate GnIH neurons. Result: hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (↓ FSH/LH → ↓ estradiol → amenorrhea). Management: Increased caloric intake, reduced training intensity, and weight restoration typically restore menses within 3-6 months. This is a form of functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA).
Vignette 7: OCP Failure & Breakthrough Bleeding
A 31-year-old woman on ethinyl estradiol/norethindrone has used OCPs reliably for 3 years without issues. Over the past 2 months, she has had breakthrough bleeding during the active pill phase. She recently started trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a UTI. She denies missed pills, vomiting, or diarrhea.
What is the most likely cause of breakthrough bleeding, and what is the mechanism?
Antibiotic-induced OCP failure. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole disrupts the normal gut flora that recirculate estrogen via enterohepatic circulation (estrobolism). Reduced estrogen reabsorption → ↓ serum estrogen levels → insufficient negative feedback to suppress FSH/LH completely → breakthrough ovulation/bleeding. Management: Continue OCP, consider additional barrier contraception during antibiotic use and for 7 days after completion, or switch to alternative antibiotic if possible. Note: Rifampin and St. John's Wort more significantly reduce OCP efficacy.
Vignette 8: Infertile Male with Isolated FSH Elevation
A 34-year-old man undergoing infertility evaluation after 2 years of unprotected intercourse reports normal erectile function and libido. Semen analysis: azoospermia (no sperm). Labs: FSH 34 mIU/L (markedly elevated), LH 7.2 mIU/L (normal), testosterone 520 ng/dL (normal), prolactin normal.
What testicular pathology is suggested by these findings?
Primary seminiferous tubule failure (Sertoli cell dysfunction/atrophy). Isolated ↑ FSH (with normal LH and testosterone) indicates: intact Leydig cell function (normal testosterone, normal LH negative feedback), but damaged Sertoli cells (↓ inhibin production → loss of FSH suppression). Causes: cryptorchidism history, prior chemotherapy, radiation, varicocele, idiopathic. Prognosis: Depends on etiology; recovery unlikely in post-testicular obstruction or severe chemical/radiation injury.
Vignette 9: Fetal Sex Determination & hCG
A pregnant woman at 10 weeks gestation asks about fetal sex determination. Her HCG level is 85,000 mIU/mL (appropriate for gestational age). What happens in the FETAL testis during this window?
What hormone is stimulating the fetal Leydig cells, and what is its source?
hCG (human Chorionic Gonadotropin) from the placental syncytiotrophoblast stimulates fetal Leydig cells to produce testosterone (and some estradiol via local aromatization). This testosterone is critical for Wolffian duct development (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles) and early genital virilization (weeks 8-14). MIS from Sertoli cells regresses Müllerian ducts. After 2nd trimester: Fetal pituitary FSH/LH take over testosterone production. hCG declines → becomes undetectable by ~12-14 weeks of fetal life.
Vignette 10: Klinefelter Syndrome Diagnosis
A 42-year-old man presents with infertility, tall stature, and mild gynecomastia. Labs: Small testes on exam (~8 mL), azoospermia on semen analysis, FSH 42 mIU/L (marked elevation), LH 18 mIU/L (elevated), testosterone 280 ng/dL (low), estradiol 52 pg/mL (elevated). Karyotype: 47,XXY.
Explain the hormone pattern in Klinefelter syndrome.
Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY): Small testes with seminiferous tubule fibrosis and Leydig cell dysfunction. ↑ FSH/LH: Primary hypogonadism (testicular failure). ↓ Testosterone: Impaired Leydig cell function. ↑ Estradiol: Reduced testosterone clearance + ↑ aromatase activity in adipose/liver. Gynecomastia: From ↑ estradiol/↓ testosterone ratio. Infertility: Azoospermia (seminiferous tubule atrophy). Management: Testosterone replacement (restores sexual function, bone health), though fertility cannot be restored without advanced techniques (TESE - testicular sperm extraction).
