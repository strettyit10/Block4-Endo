Question 1
A 55-year-old critically ill patient in the ICU has been on methylprednisolone 40 mg IV daily for 10 days for severe ARDS. Cultures come back positive for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Which immune mechanism was impaired by steroids to allow this infection?
A) Impaired neutrophil chemotaxis
B) Reduced T-cell (especially CD4+) count and impaired cell-mediated immunity
C) Decreased immunoglobulin synthesis
D) Inhibition of complement activation
E) Reduced natural killer cell count only
EXPLANATION
Glucocorticoids are powerful immunosuppressants that impair multiple immune mechanisms, most notably cell-mediated immunity. Effects include: (1) ↓T-cell proliferation and IL-2 production. (2) Increased T-cell apoptosis (especially lymphocytes in thymus and secondary lymphoid organs). (3) Lymphocyte redistribution from blood to lymphoid tissues (causing relative lymphopenia in circulating blood). (4) ↓antigen-presenting cell function. (5) ↓NK cell activity. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia is an opportunistic infection caused by a fungal organism that is normally cleared by CD4+ T-cell mediated immunity. Any significant CD4+ dysfunction (steroid-induced, HIV-related, or from other immunosuppression) increases PCP risk. Prevention: TMP-SMX (Bactrim) prophylaxis is indicated for patients on ≥20 mg/day prednisone-equivalent for >4 weeks, or for any CD4+ count <200 cells/μL.
Steroids → ↓CD4+ T cells → risk for PCP, VZV, TB reactivation, fungal infections. TMP-SMX prophylaxis indicated if ≥20 mg prednisone for >4 weeks.
Question 2
A 45-year-old executive with chronic work stress presents for insomnia and cognitive difficulties. Lab: morning cortisol 28 mcg/dL (mildly elevated), late-night salivary cortisol elevated. Which brain structure is most vulnerable to chronic elevated cortisol?
A) Amygdala — increases in size
B) Hippocampus — glucocorticoid receptor-rich; chronic exposure → atrophy and reduced neurogenesis
C) Prefrontal cortex — enhances function
D) Cerebellum
E) Striatum
EXPLANATION
The hippocampus is exquisitely sensitive to glucocorticoid excess because it expresses very high levels of glucocorticoid receptors (GR) — the highest density of any brain region. Chronic hypercortisolism causes: (1) Excitotoxic neuronal damage (excessive glutamate signaling). (2) Reduced BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) → impaired neuronal survival. (3) Suppressed neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus (where new neurons are normally generated). (4) Overall hippocampal volume reduction visible on MRI. Clinical consequences: impaired hippocampal-dependent memory (declarative/explicit memory), depression, anxiety, and cognitive difficulties. In contrast, the amygdala (which expresses lower levels of GR) is relatively spared or even hyper-responsive to stress, contributing to heightened fear and anxiety responses. This dissociation (amygdala hyperactivity + hippocampal hypoactivity) is characteristic of depression and PTSD.
Cortisol → hippocampal atrophy (high GR density). Memory impairment in Cushing syndrome and chronic stress. Amygdala: activated (anxiety ↑). Hippocampus: damaged (memory ↓).
Question 3
Cytokines released during systemic infection trigger CRH secretion. This represents which physiological principle?
A) Immune system activation suppresses HPA axis
B) Immune-to-neuroendocrine communication: IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α → hypothalamus → CRH → ACTH → cortisol, which then suppresses excessive inflammation
C) CRH activates cytokine production
D) Cortisol amplifies TNF-α effects
E) This represents a positive feedback loop that perpetuates infection
EXPLANATION
The integration of immune and neuroendocrine systems during infection is essential for survival. Mechanism: (1) Infection triggers innate immune response → macrophages and dendritic cells produce IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α. (2) These cytokines reach the brain via: (a) direct circumventricular organ (OVLT, median eminence) stimulation, (b) vagal afferent signaling, (c) crossing the blood-brain barrier during inflammation. (3) Cytokines stimulate hypothalamic neurons → ↑CRH release → pituitary → ↑ACTH → adrenal → ↑cortisol. (4) Cortisol acts as a brake: suppresses further cytokine production (↓IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), stabilizes mast cells (↓histamine), inhibits phospholipase A2 (↓arachidonic acid → ↓prostaglandins/leukotrienes). This forms a negative feedback loop that prevents cytokine storm and shock. Patients with adrenal insufficiency cannot mount adequate cortisol response → exaggerated cytokine responses → septic shock. This is why stress-dose steroids are given in septic shock.
Cytokines → CRH → cortisol → anti-inflammatory. Negative feedback brake on inflammation. Adrenal insufficiency = impaired stress response → cytokine storm risk.
Question 4
A 24-year-old female elite runner with BMI 17 has not had a menstrual period in 9 months. FSH is 2.4, LH is 1.8, estradiol is low. Which mechanism best explains the amenorrhea?
A) Primary ovarian failure
B) Prolactinoma
C) Elevated CRH from chronic stress/energy deficit → suppresses GnRH pulse generator
D) Direct estrogen deficiency from adipose loss
E) Elevated cortisol suppresses FSH but not LH
EXPLANATION
Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA), a component of the Female Athlete Triad / Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S): The root cause is energy deficiency (inadequate caloric intake relative to energy expenditure). Energy deficit activates CRH neurons in the hypothalamus → ↑CRH tone + ↑cortisol. CRH acts on GnRH neurons → direct inhibition of pulsatile GnRH release. Additionally, cortisol suppresses HPG axis function. Result: Loss of pulsatile LH/FSH secretion → low LH, low FSH, low estradiol → amenorrhea. Key: BOTH LH and FSH are low (distinguishes from primary ovarian failure, where FSH would be high). Treatment: address the root cause (energy deficit) by increasing caloric intake and reducing training intensity. Simply giving OCPs masks the underlying problem but does not restore menstrual function or prevent bone loss progression. RED-S expanded concept includes male athletes with hypogonadism from energy deficiency.
FHA: CRH + cortisol suppress GnRH → low LH + low FSH + low estrogen. FSH NOT elevated (distinguishes from ovarian failure). Address energy deficit first.
Question 5
A 28-year-old woman begins high-dose corticosteroids for an autoimmune condition. After 6 months, she develops vertebral compression fractures. What is the PRIMARY mechanism of glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis?
A) Increase in bone resorption via direct osteoclast activation
B) Multiple mechanisms: ↓osteoblast differentiation/function (direct GR effect), ↑RANKL/↓OPG (↑osteoclasts), ↓intestinal calcium absorption, ↑renal calcium excretion, ↓sex hormones
C) Glucocorticoids block vitamin D activation in the liver
D) ↑PTH from secondary hyperparathyroidism only
E) Increased cortical bone loss without trabecular involvement
EXPLANATION
Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) involves multiple pathogenic mechanisms: (1) PRIMARY: Osteoblast dysfunction. GR in osteoblasts → ↓Runx2 transcription factor → ↓osteoblast differentiation and recruitment. Additionally, GC increase osteoblast apoptosis → loss of mature bone-forming cells. Result: severely impaired bone formation. (2) Osteoclast activation: GC increase RANKL expression + decrease OPG → ↑osteoclast formation and activity → bone resorption. (3) Calcium metabolism: GC antagonize vitamin D action → ↓intestinal calcium absorption, ↑renal calcium wasting → secondary hyperparathyroidism. (4) Sex hormone deficiency: GC suppress GnRH → ↓LH/FSH → ↓estrogen/testosterone → additional bone loss. Most rapid bone loss occurs in first 3-6 months (while on steroids). Prevention and treatment: (1) Minimize steroid dose. (2) Calcium 1200 mg/day + Vitamin D 800-1000 IU/day (standard). (3) Bisphosphonate (e.g., alendronate) — significantly reduces fracture risk. (4) Monitor BMD with DXA scan. (5) Weight-bearing exercise.
GC osteoporosis: multiple mechanisms. ↓osteoblast + ↑osteoclast + ↓calcium absorption. Fastest bone loss = first 3-6 months. Bisphosphonate + Ca + VitD for prevention.
Question 6
Which stress-related mediator directly promotes GI stress ulcer formation in critically ill patients?
A) Elevated serotonin from intestinal cells
B) Catecholamines → splanchnic vasoconstriction + cortisol → ↓mucosal prostaglandins → mucosal breakdown
C) ACTH-stimulated aldosterone → GI epithelium damage
D) CRH directly damages gastric mucosa via CRH receptors
E) Elevated glucagon from counter-regulatory response
EXPLANATION
Stress ulcers (acute erosive gastritis in critically ill patients) develop from combined ischemic and cytoprotective defect mechanisms: (1) Ischemic component: Severe stress activates sympathetic nervous system → catecholamine surge → α1-adrenergic vasoconstriction of splanchnic vessels → ↓mucosal blood flow → mucosal ischemia → epithelial damage. (2) Loss of mucosal defenses: Cortisol (elevated in critical illness) → ↓prostaglandin E2 synthesis (inhibits phospholipase A2 and COX) → reduced protective mucus layer + reduced HCO3- secretion → loss of acid neutralization and physical protection. (3) Result: Ischemic epithelium + loss of defensive barriers → erosion and hemorrhage. Risk factors: mechanical ventilation >48h, severe sepsis, coagulopathy, severe burns, head/spinal injury, acute liver failure. Prevention: (1) PPI (superior to H2 blockers). (2) Early enteral nutrition (maintains mucosal integrity). (3) Correction of coagulopathy. (4) Blood pressure support.
Stress ulcer: catecholamines (ischemia) + cortisol (↓PGE2 → ↓mucus). Prevention: PPI in high-risk ICU patients (MV >48h, coagulopathy, burns).
Question 7
Chronic psychological stress activates the β2-adrenergic receptor on tumor cells. What downstream effect promotes tumor metastasis?
A) ↓VEGF and ↓MMP
B) β2-AR → Gs → ↑cAMP → PKA → ↑VEGF + ↑MMP (matrix invasion) + ↑EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition)
C) β2-AR → Gi → ↓cAMP → ↓apoptosis
D) Catecholamines block NK cell activation
E) β2-AR → PLC → IP3 → tumor angiogenesis
EXPLANATION
Chronic psychological stress activates the sympathetic nervous system, increasing circulating and tissue catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine). These bind β2-adrenergic receptors on tumor cells: β2-AR → Gs protein → adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA phosphorylation → transcriptional changes. Downstream effects promoting metastasis: (1) ↑VEGF expression → angiogenesis → new blood vessel formation → increased blood supply to tumor → nutrient delivery and escape route. (2) ↑MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression → matrix metalloproteinases degrade extracellular matrix and basement membrane → facilitates invasion and extravasation. (3) ↑EMT transcription factors (Snail, Slug) → epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal characteristics → migration capacity, invasiveness. Additionally: cortisol from concurrent HPA activation → ↓NK cell count, ↓cytokine production, ↓NK cell-mediated tumor killing → impaired immune surveillance. Clinical relevance: β-blockers (especially non-selective propranolol) being studied as potential adjunct therapy to reduce stress-mediated tumor progression. This has major implications for counseling cancer patients on stress reduction.
Chronic stress → catecholamines → β2-AR → cAMP → VEGF + MMP + EMT = pro-metastatic. Propranolol under study. Also: cortisol → ↓NK cells → tumor escape.
Question 8
Glucocorticoids induce apoptosis in thymocytes and lymphocytes. What is the primary molecular mechanism?
A) Blocking TNF receptor signaling
B) GR-mediated transcription of BIM (pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family member) → mitochondrial apoptosis pathway
C) GR blocks anti-apoptotic BCL-2 at the membrane
D) Direct caspase-8 activation via death receptor
E) ↓FLIP (inhibitor of caspase-8) expression
EXPLANATION
Glucocorticoid-induced apoptosis in lymphocytes and thymocytes is a well-characterized mechanism: Cortisol → glucocorticoid receptor (GR) in cytoplasm → GR-ligand complex → nuclear translocation → GR-GRE interaction → transactivation of pro-apoptotic genes. The primary target is BIM (BCL-2 interacting mediator of cell death), a BH3-only pro-apoptotic member of the BCL-2 family. BIM upregulation → interaction with anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2, BCL-xL) → activation of pro-apoptotic BAX and BAK → mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) → cytochrome c release → cytosolic apoptosome formation → caspase-9 activation → executioner caspase-3 → apoptotic cell death. Thymocytes are exceptionally sensitive to GC-induced apoptosis (single-positive and double-positive cells eliminated). This mechanism is therapeutically exploited: prednisone is a backbone component of CHOP chemotherapy for Hodgkin lymphoma; dexamethasone is used in multiple myeloma and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Clinical consequence: pronounced lymphopenia during prolonged corticosteroid therapy (especially CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell counts drop), increasing infection risk.
GC → BIM (pro-apoptotic) → mitochondrial apoptosis pathway → lymphocyte/thymocyte death. Therapeutic basis for prednisone in lymphoma. Clinically: lymphopenia on steroids.
BRS Question 1
During the acute stress response, the sympatho-adrenal medullary (SAM) axis is activated. What are the immediate cardiovascular effects of catecholamine release?
A) ↓Heart rate, ↑peripheral resistance, vasodilation
B) ↑Heart rate (β1), ↑cardiac output, ↑peripheral resistance (α1), splanchnic vasoconstriction, skeletal muscle vasodilation (β2)
C) Bradycardia and hypotension
D) Peripheral vasodilation only
E) Increased aldosterone directly from catecholamines
EXPLANATION
The sympatho-adrenal medullary (SAM) axis provides immediate (seconds) physiological response to acute stress. Pathway: Stressor → hypothalamus → sympathetic preganglionic neurons (via LC and other centers) → adrenal medulla → catecholamine release (epinephrine 80%, norepinephrine 20%). Adrenergic effects: (1) β1-AR on heart: ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑AV nodal conduction. (2) α1-AR on blood vessels: vasoconstriction in skin, GI, renal circulation (splanchnic vasoconstriction). (3) β2-AR on blood vessels in skeletal muscle: vasodilation (↑blood flow to muscles). (4) Net effect: ↑cardiac output, ↑blood pressure, perfusion shifted to muscles/brain. Metabolic: β2-AR on liver/muscle → glycogenolysis; β2-AR on adipose → lipolysis. Pancreas: β2 on beta cells → ↓insulin; α1 on alpha cells → ↑glucagon. Result: mobilization of glucose and fatty acids. The SAM response is fast but short-lived. The HPA response (minutes to hours) provides sustained metabolic support.
SAM: immediate (seconds). Catecholamines → ↑HR + ↑CO + ↑glycogenolysis. HPA: delayed (hours). Cortisol → sustains metabolic response.
BRS Question 2
Glucocorticoids are used therapeutically for many inflammatory conditions. What is the primary mechanism of their anti-inflammatory effect at the cellular level?
A) Upregulation of prostaglandin synthase
B) Transrepression of NF-κB → ↓IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2 gene expression
C) Direct mast cell membrane stabilization via Gs pathway
D) Activation of complement cascade inhibitors
E) Stimulation of IL-10 only
EXPLANATION
The anti-inflammatory potency of glucocorticoids derives from two main mechanisms: (1) TRANSREPRESSION (accounts for ~70% of effects): GR in cytoplasm → nuclear translocation → physical interaction with NF-κB and AP-1 transcription factors (without binding DNA itself) → blocks their ability to activate target genes → ↓IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2, iNOS, ICAM-1, selectins, chemokines. This is why GC are so effective at suppressing inflammation. (2) TRANSACTIVATION (accounts for ~30% of effects): GR-GRE interaction on DNA → ↑annexin A1 (lipocortin-1) → inhibits phospholipase A2 → ↓arachidonic acid release → ↓prostaglandins/leukotrienes; ↑IκBα → sequesters NF-κB; ↑gluconeogenic and metabolic genes. Both mechanisms contribute, but transrepression is the primary anti-inflammatory mechanism. This also explains why GC have immunosuppressive effects beyond simple anti-inflammatory action.
Primary anti-inflammatory: GR transrepression of NF-κB → ↓IL-1/6, TNF-α, COX-2. Transactivation: ↑annexin A1 (↓phospholipase A2). Both contribute.
BRS Question 3
Psychosocial stress activates the HPA axis. What type of stress is MOST effective at activating ACTH/cortisol secretion?
A) Acute physical pain alone
B) Anticipatory anxiety and uncontrollability — psychological stressors where the outcome is uncertain and uncontrollable
C) Brief, controllable acute stress
D) Post-exercise recovery
E) Sleep deprivation alone
EXPLANATION
HPA axis activation by stress is strongly modulated by cognitive appraisal: The same physical stressor activates the HPA axis more if it is UNCONTROLLABLE or UNPREDICTABLE, and less if it is CONTROLLABLE or PREDICTABLE. Classic animal studies (Weiss rat model): rats exposed to unpredictable, uncontrollable shock developed greater HPA activation and hippocampal damage than rats receiving identical shocks but with control (ability to escape). Psychological stress types that maximally activate HPA: (1) Anticipatory anxiety (uncertainty about outcome). (2) Loss of control (uncontrollability). (3) Social threat (public speaking, social evaluation). (4) Cognitive challenge. Physical stressors (exercise, cold, pain) preferentially activate SAM (immediate, short-lived catecholamine response) over HPA (slower, sustained cortisol response). Chronic uncontrollable/unpredictable stress → sustained HPA elevation → hippocampal neuronal damage → cognitive impairment, depression, anxiety. This has clinical implications: patients with perceived lack of control in medical situations show higher cortisol responses; explaining procedures and giving autonomy reduces stress response.
Psychological uncontrollability → maximal HPA activation. Physical stress → maximal SAM. Chronic uncontrollable stress → hippocampal damage + PTSD-related HPA dysregulation.
BRS Question 4
A patient taking prednisone 30 mg/day is found to have blood glucose of 220 mg/dL. He was not diabetic before. What mechanism explains steroid-induced hyperglycemia?
A) Prednisone directly destroys beta cells
B) Glucocorticoids upregulate PEPCK and G6Pase (gluconeogenesis) + cause peripheral insulin resistance via IRS-1 serine phosphorylation
C) Cortisol activates glucagon receptors
D) Cortisol inhibits insulin secretion via V2 receptor
E) Prednisone inhibits glucose transporter GLUT2 in liver
EXPLANATION
Glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycemia results from two primary mechanisms: (1) HEPATIC (predominant): GR → GRE interaction → ↑PEPCK and G6Pase gene transcription → increased gluconeogenesis from lactate, amino acids, glycerol → increased hepatic glucose output (can increase 2-3 fold). (2) PERIPHERAL INSULIN RESISTANCE: In muscle, GC → ↓GLUT4 translocation + IRS-1 serine phosphorylation (instead of tyrosine) → uncoupling of insulin signaling → ↓PI3K/Akt activation → ↓glucose uptake. In adipose, GC → ↑hormone-sensitive lipase → ↑lipolysis → ↑free fatty acids → compete with glucose for oxidation (Randle cycle) → further hepatic insulin resistance. (3) Mild ↓insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. Clinical: Hyperglycemia on steroids is often worst postprandially (glucose rises higher after meals) because the liver produces excess glucose while muscles cannot take it up efficiently. Management: monitor glucose; may require antidiabetic therapy (metformin, GLP-1 agonist, or insulin). Highest risk: pre-existing diabetes, obesity, high-dose steroids.
Steroid hyperglycemia: ↑hepatic gluconeogenesis (PEPCK/G6Pase) + peripheral insulin resistance. Typically highest postprandially (afternoon/evening with morning prednisone). Treat with insulin if needed.
BRS Question 5
The female athlete triad has three components. Which is considered the root cause driving the other two?
A) Bone stress fractures
B) Low bone mineral density
C) Menstrual dysfunction
D) Low energy availability (energy deficiency)
E) High cortisol
EXPLANATION
The Female Athlete Triad consists of three interrelated but causally linked components, with LOW ENERGY AVAILABILITY as the root cause: (1) Low energy availability (energy deficiency): Inadequate caloric intake relative to energy expenditure from exercise. This triggers a cascade of metabolic and neuroendocrine changes. (2) Menstrual dysfunction: Energy deficit → increased CRH tone (CRH neurons are sensitive to energy status) → CRH inhibits GnRH neurons → suppression of pulsatile GnRH → ↓LH/FSH → ↓estradiol → primary amenorrhea or secondary amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea. (3) Low bone mineral density: ↓estrogen (from suppressed HPG axis) → ↓bone formation, ↑bone resorption. Additionally, poor energy status means insufficient substrate for osteoblast function. Result: progressive bone loss and stress fractures. Clinical management: The KEY is to address the ROOT CAUSE (energy deficit) by: (1) Increasing caloric intake. (2) Reducing exercise intensity/duration. (3) Counseling about RED-S. Simply giving OCPs (oral contraceptive pills) treats the menstrual symptom but does NOT address energy deficit, does NOT reverse HPA dysregulation, does NOT prevent bone loss progression. The updated terminology "Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport" (RED-S) encompasses this concept and includes male athletes with hypogonadism from energy deficiency. Male RED-S: low energy availability → ↑CRH → ↓GnRH → ↓testosterone → hypogonadism.
Triad root cause: energy deficiency. Fix energy deficiency → reverses menstrual dysfunction → protects bone. OCP treats symptoms but does not fix root cause.
