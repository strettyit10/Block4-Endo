L5 · Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis

Practice Questions

Question 1
A 38-year-old woman with central obesity, purple striae, and hypertension has 24-hour urinary free cortisol of 4× upper limit of normal. Plasma ACTH is 95 pg/mL (elevated). MRI pituitary shows a 7 mm adenoma. What is the diagnosis?
A) Adrenal adenoma
B) Ectopic ACTH syndrome
C) Cushing disease (pituitary ACTH adenoma)
D) Primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease
E) Exogenous glucocorticoid use
EXPLANATION
Cushing disease is a pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma causing ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. The triad of elevated ACTH, pituitary adenoma on MRI, and high cortisol confirms pituitary source. Classic features include central obesity, purple striae, easy bruising, proximal muscle weakness, hypertension, glucose intolerance, and osteoporosis. Treatment is transsphenoidal surgery. Adrenal sources suppress ACTH via feedback inhibition.
Cushing disease = pituitary corticotroph adenoma. ACTH elevated + pituitary lesion = Cushing disease. ACTH suppressed = adrenal source.
Question 2
A 62-year-old man with SCLC develops muscle weakness, hypokalemia (K 2.6 mEq/L), metabolic alkalosis, and hypertension. Plasma cortisol is 85 mcg/dL. ACTH is 980 pg/mL. High-dose dexamethasone (8 mg) does not suppress cortisol. What is the diagnosis?
A) Cushing disease
B) Primary adrenal carcinoma
C) Ectopic ACTH syndrome
D) Iatrogenic Cushing
E) Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia independent of ACTH
EXPLANATION
Ectopic ACTH syndrome presents with extremely high ACTH (often >1000 pg/mL), extremely high cortisol, and no suppression on HDDST because ectopic tumor cells lack normal glucocorticoid receptors for feedback. Severe hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis occurs because cortisol at such high levels overwhelms 11β-HSD2 (cortisol inactivation enzyme), allowing cortisol to activate mineralocorticoid receptors, causing potassium wasting and sodium retention. SCLC is the most common source. Rapid onset without classic Cushingoid features (moon facies, striae) due to acute presentation. Treatment: address underlying malignancy + medical adrenal blockade (ketoconazole, metyrapone, etomidate).
Ectopic ACTH: very HIGH ACTH (often >1000 pg/mL), no HDDST suppression, severe hypokalemic alkalosis. SCLC most common. No classic features = rapid course. Medical blockade required.
Question 3
A 42-year-old woman recently treated with prednisone 40 mg/day for 6 weeks for an inflammatory condition develops shock following dental surgery. She had tapered and stopped steroids 5 days prior. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Septic shock from dental infection
B) Anaphylaxis to anesthetic
C) Adrenal crisis — HPA suppression from recent steroids
D) Pulmonary embolism
E) Vagal syncope
EXPLANATION
Adrenal crisis in this context results from HPA axis suppression caused by prolonged high-dose glucocorticoid therapy. After stopping steroids, the HPA axis takes weeks to months to recover because the adrenal cortex has atrophied from lack of ACTH stimulation. When surgical stress occurs during this recovery window, the suppressed adrenal gland cannot produce sufficient cortisol to meet the increased demand, leading to shock. Management: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg STAT followed by fluid resuscitation. Prevention: perioperative stress-dose steroids (hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV) for any major stress within 6-12 months of prolonged steroid use.
HPA recovery after steroids: weeks-months. Adrenal stress response blunted during recovery. Stress-dose steroids needed for surgery within 6-12 months of prolonged steroid use. Always screen for recent steroid exposure perioperatively.
Question 4
Which of the following is the most appropriate initial test to screen for hypercortisolism in a patient suspected of having Cushing syndrome?
A) Random serum cortisol
B) 24-hour urinary free cortisol (UFC)
C) Serum ACTH level
D) High-dose dexamethasone test
E) Pituitary MRI
EXPLANATION
The diagnostic approach to Cushing syndrome follows a stepwise pattern: (1) Screening: confirm hypercortisolism using 24-hour UFC, late-night salivary cortisol, or 1 mg overnight DST. Need ≥2 abnormal tests. (2) Source determination: ACTH level to determine ACTH-dependent vs independent. (3) Localization: HDDST or IPSS for ACTH-dependent; imaging for ACTH-independent. 24-hour UFC is highly sensitive for overt Cushing syndrome.
Cushing screening: 24h UFC, late-night salivary cortisol, 1mg ONDST (any 2 abnormal = confirmed). THEN ACTH (source), THEN HDDST/IPSS (localization).
Question 5
A newborn is found to have ambiguous genitalia, hyponatremia (Na 128), hyperkalemia (K 6.5), and hypoglycemia. 17-hydroxyprogesterone is markedly elevated. What is the most likely enzyme deficiency?
A) 17α-hydroxylase
B) 11β-hydroxylase
C) 21-hydroxylase
D) 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
E) Aldosterone synthase
EXPLANATION
21-hydroxylase deficiency is the most common form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (90%). This enzyme is critical for both cortisol and aldosterone synthesis. Deficiency causes: (1) ↓Cortisol → hypoglycemia + adrenal insufficiency symptoms. (2) ↓Aldosterone → salt-wasting crisis (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, shock). (3) ↑17-OHP and ↑DHEA (substrate accumulation) → androgen excess → virilization (ambiguous genitalia in females, precocious puberty in males). Diagnosis is confirmed by markedly elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone (>1000 ng/dL in salt-wasting form). Treatment requires both glucocorticoid (hydrocortisone) and mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone) replacement.
21-OH deficiency: salt-wasting (↓aldo) + virilization (↑androgens) + ↓cortisol + ↑17-OHP. Most common CAH. Classic triad: hypoNa + hyperK + ambiguous genitalia in females.
Question 6
Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) is performed in a patient with ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. The result shows a central:peripheral ACTH ratio of 4.2 after CRH stimulation. What does this indicate?
A) Ectopic ACTH source
B) Primary adrenal Cushing
C) Pituitary source (Cushing disease)
D) Normal finding
E) Inconclusive — bilateral sampling failed
EXPLANATION
Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) is the gold standard test for localizing ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome when MRI is negative or equivocal. Central blood (from pituitary) is compared to peripheral blood ACTH. Interpretation: Central:peripheral ratio ≥2 at baseline or ≥3 after CRH stimulation = pituitary source (Cushing disease). Ratio <2 = ectopic ACTH source. CRH stimulation during IPSS significantly improves sensitivity. This patient's ratio of 4.2 after CRH (>3) definitively confirms pituitary Cushing disease. IPSS also allows lateralization (left vs right inferior petrosal sinus) to guide surgical approach.
IPSS: ratio ≥2 basal / ≥3 after CRH = pituitary source. Ratio <2 = ectopic. Used when MRI negative or equivocal — gold standard localization.
Question 7
A patient with primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease) presents to the ER with hypotension, confusion, vomiting, and temperature 39°C precipitated by a GI infection. What is the immediate management?
A) Oral hydrocortisone dose doubling
B) Fludrocortisone 0.1 mg PO
C) IV hydrocortisone 100 mg bolus + 0.9% saline 1-2L
D) Methylprednisolone 500 mg IV for anti-inflammatory effect
E) ACTH stimulation test before starting treatment
EXPLANATION
Adrenal crisis is a medical emergency requiring immediate, empirical treatment without waiting for lab confirmation. Treatment protocol: (1) IV hydrocortisone 100 mg STAT (has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid potency at this dose). (2) IV normal saline 1-2L for volume resuscitation and sodium correction. (3) Continue hydrocortisone 50-75 mg IV q6-8h. The high dose of hydrocortisone provides sufficient mineralocorticoid activity at the mineralocorticoid receptor, so fludrocortisone is not needed acutely (though it is added back at lower maintenance steroid doses). After stabilization, transition to maintenance: hydrocortisone 15-20 mg/day split dosing + fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily.
Adrenal crisis: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg STAT + NS. Never delay for labs. High-dose hydrocortisone = acts on mineralocorticoid receptors → no fludrocortisone needed acutely.
Question 8
A patient with Cushing disease (pituitary corticotroph adenoma) undergoes bilateral adrenalectomy. 18 months later she develops very dark skin, headaches, and visual field loss. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Addison disease from adrenalectomy
B) Nelson syndrome
C) Pituitary apoplexy
D) Secondary Cushing from adrenal remnant
E) Recurrent ACTH adenoma with normal adrenal response
EXPLANATION
Nelson syndrome develops when the negative feedback inhibition of cortisol on a pituitary ACTH adenoma is removed by bilateral adrenalectomy. Without cortisol feedback, the corticotroph adenoma grows aggressively, producing extremely high levels of ACTH (often >1000 pg/mL). The ACTH molecule contains an MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone) sequence that activates melanocyte-stimulating hormone receptors, causing hyperpigmentation (dark skin). Mass effect from tumor growth compresses nearby structures (optic chiasm → visual field loss, brainstem → headache). Prevention: prophylactic pituitary radiation before bilateral adrenalectomy reduces Nelson syndrome risk from ~8% to ~2%. Post-adrenalectomy surveillance: serial ACTH levels and pituitary MRI monitoring. Treatment: transsphenoidal pituitary surgery ± external beam radiation.
Nelson: post-bilateral adrenalectomy → loss of cortisol feedback → ACTH adenoma growth → hyperpigmentation (ACTH/MSH) + mass effect. Prevention = pituitary radiation.
Question 9
A patient with suspected Cushing disease undergoes high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (8 mg over 2 days). Cortisol suppresses by 60% from baseline. What does this suggest?
A) Ectopic ACTH source
B) Primary adrenal Cushing
C) Pituitary Cushing disease
D) Exogenous steroid use
E) Normal variation — cannot distinguish
EXPLANATION
The high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (HDDST) helps distinguish pituitary (Cushing disease) from ectopic or adrenal sources of ACTH-dependent Cushing. Interpretation: >50% suppression of cortisol = pituitary source (Cushing disease). The pituitary corticotroph adenoma retains some negative feedback responsiveness to high-dose glucocorticoids. No suppression (<50%) = ectopic ACTH (ectopic tumor cells often lack glucocorticoid receptors) or primary adrenal adenoma (ACTH already suppressed). The HDDST has limitations (sensitivity and specificity ~80%). IPSS is the more accurate localizing test when MRI is negative or equivocal.
HDDST: >50% suppression = pituitary. No suppression = ectopic or adrenal. Sensitivity 80% — use IPSS for definitive localization.
Question 10
Describe the 3-day metyrapone test and its interpretation in the evaluation of pituitary Cushing disease.
A) Metyrapone blocks ACTH synthesis; elevated ACTH confirms pituitary adenoma
B) Metyrapone blocks 11β-hydroxylase → ↓cortisol → removes negative feedback → ↑ACTH + ↑11-deoxycortisol. Pituitary Cushing: exaggerated ACTH and 11-deoxycortisol response
C) Metyrapone stimulates CRH directly; excess CRH confirms hypothalamic Cushing
D) Metyrapone suppresses aldosterone; positive test = CAH
E) Metyrapone is used only for secondary adrenal insufficiency testing
EXPLANATION
The metyrapone test is a dynamic challenge test used diagnostically and therapeutically. Metyrapone is an 11β-hydroxylase inhibitor that blocks the final step of cortisol synthesis. When cortisol falls → negative feedback removed → CRH/ACTH rise. Interpretation: (1) Normal/pituitary Cushing: exaggerated rise in ACTH and 11-deoxycortisol (the substrate that accumulates). (2) Adrenal Cushing: blunted or flat response (ACTH already suppressed by autonomous cortisol production). (3) Adrenal insufficiency: flat response (no ACTH response capacity). The 11-deoxycortisol (the product of the blocked step) is measured. A positive metyrapone test shows an exaggerated rise. Therapeutically, metyrapone is used for short-term control of severe hypercortisolism while planning definitive surgery or radiation.
Metyrapone → ↓cortisol → ↑ACTH + ↑11-deoxycortisol. Exaggerated = pituitary/normal HPA. Flat = adrenal insufficiency. Used diagnostically and for short-term symptom management.
BRS Question 1
CRH stimulates corticotrophs to produce ACTH. What is the primary second messenger used by CRH in corticotrophs?
B) cAMP → PKA
A) IP3 → calcium
C) cGMP
D) DAG → PKC
E) Direct nuclear GR activation
EXPLANATION
CRH exerts its effects through CRHR1 (corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor 1), a G-protein coupled receptor on corticotroph cells. CRH → CRHR1 → Gs-mediated activation of adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA phosphorylation of transcription factors (CREB) → POMC gene transcription → ACTH synthesis and release. AVP also signals to corticotrophs (via V1b and V3 receptors) → Gq → PKC pathway, and AVP synergizes with CRH for potent stimulation during acute stress. This dual pathway (CRH + AVP) allows for graded ACTH responses.
CRH → Gs → cAMP (primary). AVP → V1b → Gq → PKC (synergistic). Together → maximum ACTH response during severe stress.
BRS Question 2
Aldosterone is synthesized primarily in which zone of the adrenal cortex?
A) Zona fasciculata
B) Zona reticularis
C) Zona glomerulosa
D) Adrenal medulla
E) Zona intermedia
EXPLANATION
The adrenal cortex has three zones with distinct hormone synthesis: (1) Zona glomerulosa (outermost) = mineralocorticoids (aldosterone). (2) Zona fasciculata (middle, largest) = glucocorticoids (cortisol). (3) Zona reticularis (innermost) = adrenal androgens (DHEA, DHEA-S, androstenedione). Mnemonic: "GFR" from outside-in = "Salt, Sugar, Sex." Aldosterone: regulated primarily by the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and hyperkalemia. ACTH has a minor role in aldosterone regulation.
Zona G = aldosterone. Zona F = cortisol. Zona R = androgens. GFR = Salt, Sugar, Sex. Aldosterone controlled by RAAS, not primarily ACTH.
BRS Question 3
What is the normal diurnal variation of ACTH and cortisol, and how is this used in the diagnosis of Cushing syndrome?
A) Peak at midnight; used to detect low midnight cortisol
B) Peak at 6-8 AM; absence of diurnal variation (high midnight cortisol/salivary cortisol) is a marker of Cushing syndrome
C) No diurnal variation — random cortisol is reliable
D) Peak at noon; late-morning hypocortisolemia suggests Cushing
E) Peak at 3 AM; 6 AM cortisol is the reference standard
EXPLANATION
The HPA axis displays circadian rhythm controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Normal pattern: (1) Peak ACTH and cortisol at 6-8 AM (early morning awakening spike). (2) Gradual decline throughout day. (3) Nadir at midnight (when asleep). This rhythm is driven by clock genes and CRH release. In Cushing syndrome: loss of normal diurnal variation → cortisol elevated at midnight when it should be suppressed. This is exploited diagnostically: late-night salivary cortisol (11 PM–midnight) normally suppressed to <7.5 nmol/L. If elevated on ≥2 occasions, it indicates hypercortisolism (Cushing syndrome). Depressed patients, shift workers, and alcoholics can have "pseudo-Cushing" with loss of diurnal variation without true hypercortisolism.
Cortisol nadir = midnight. Late-night salivary cortisol elevated = Cushing (loss of diurnal rhythm). Peak = 6-8 AM. 1mg ONDST given at midnight → checks 8 AM cortisol suppression.
BRS Question 4
Cortisol inhibits ACTH secretion via negative feedback at the pituitary and hypothalamus. What is the cellular mechanism at the pituitary?
A) Cortisol binds membrane receptor → activates Gi → ↓cAMP
B) Cortisol binds nuclear glucocorticoid receptor → ↓POMC gene transcription + ↓CRH receptor expression
C) Cortisol stimulates somatostatin release within the pituitary
D) Cortisol activates dopamine D2 receptors on corticotrophs
E) Cortisol directly blocks ACTH secretory vesicle exocytosis
EXPLANATION
Cortisol negative feedback operates through nuclear glucocorticoid receptors (GR) at both the pituitary and hypothalamus. At the pituitary corticotroph: (1) Slow feedback (transcriptional): cortisol-GR complex → ↓POMC gene transcription → ↓ACTH synthesis. Cortisol also ↓CRH receptor expression → corticotrophs less responsive to CRH stimulation. (2) Fast feedback (non-genomic): cortisol acutely inhibits ACTH secretory vesicle exocytosis via membrane mechanisms. At the hypothalamus: cortisol → ↓CRH gene expression + ↓AVP gene expression. This multi-level feedback system maintains HPA axis homeostasis. Dysregulation of this feedback (loss of negative feedback) is key to Cushing syndrome pathogenesis.
Cortisol feedback: nuclear GR → ↓POMC (↓ACTH) + ↓CRH-R expression. Both fast (exocytosis inhibition) and slow (gene transcription) mechanisms involved.
BRS Question 5
A 65-year-old on prednisone 20 mg/day for 3 months needs to undergo major elective surgery. How should perioperative steroids be managed?
A) Discontinue prednisone 2 weeks before surgery
B) Continue home dose only — no additional steroids needed
C) Administer stress-dose steroids: hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV before surgery, then taper over 24-48 hours
D) Give methylprednisolone 1 g IV for 3 days perioperatively
E) Switch to dexamethasone the night before surgery
EXPLANATION
Perioperative steroid management in patients with HPA axis suppression: Any patient on ≥10 mg/day prednisone-equivalent for ≥3 weeks has suppressed HPA axis and adrenal atrophy. Major surgery represents severe stress. Normal adrenal cortex secretes 75-150 mg cortisol-equivalent daily during major surgical stress — vastly more than the patient's home replacement dose. Management: (1) Hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV before anesthetic induction (peak level achieved within minutes). (2) Continue hydrocortisone 25-50 mg IV q8h for 24-48 hours post-op. (3) Taper back to baseline maintenance dose. Hydrocortisone is preferred because at high doses it has both glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid potency. Failure to provide stress-dose steroids perioperatively risks intraoperative adrenal crisis and cardiovascular collapse.
Major surgery + ≥3 weeks steroids = stress dose needed. Hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV before surgery. Taper over 24-48h. Failure = adrenal crisis risk.
