Vignette 1L1 + L2 — GPCR signaling + Adrenal
A 14-year-old girl presents with precocious puberty, café-au-lait spots, and polyostotic fibrous dysplasia. Her cortisol is elevated but ACTH is also elevated (should be low if adrenal).
→ Explain why McCune-Albright affects both the gonads AND adrenal simultaneously, and why ACTH is elevated rather than suppressed.
✅ Full Mechanistic Answer
McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS): Somatic gain-of-function mutation in GNAS (Gs α-subunit) → constitutively active Gs in multiple tissues (gonads, adrenal, bone).

Gonadal involvement: Gs mutation → ↑cAMP → LH receptor activation independent of LH → precocious puberty.

Adrenal involvement: Gs mutation in fasciculata cells → ↑cAMP → constitutive cortisol production. But here's the twist: autonomous cortisol production occurs WITHOUT ACTH-driven stimulation initially. However, some adrenal cells also express GNAS mutation → constitutive Gs activation in pituitary CRH pathway → ↑CRH from hypothalamus → ↑ACTH despite high cortisol (explaining the inappropriately elevated ACTH).

Pathophysiology chain: Gs (constitutive ON) → ↑cAMP → PKA → StAR → cholesterol import → cortisol synthesis in adrenal. In pituitary: Gs ON → loss of normal feedback inhibition → continued ACTH secretion.
Combines L1 (Gs coupling, constitutive activation) with L2 (adrenal steroidogenesis, ACTH feedback). Tests whether you understand somatic mutations create tissue-specific constitutive activation.
Vignette 2L2 + L3 — Adrenal + Sex Steroid Pathways
A 46,XY newborn with ambiguous genitalia presents with no adrenal hyperplasia. Cortisol is very low, aldosterone is low, testosterone is low, DHEA is very low, 17-OHP is LOW (not elevated).
→ Which single enzyme deficiency explains all four hormone failures? Why does this affect BOTH adrenal and gonadal steroidogenesis?
✅ Answer
CYP11A1 (cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme / P450scc) or StAR deficiency.

CYP11A1 catalyzes the FIRST committed step in ALL steroidogenesis: Cholesterol → Pregnenolone. Without it, NO pregnenolone is made in any steroidogenic tissue (adrenal zona fasciculata/reticularis, Leydig cells, ovaries).

Result: Every downstream hormone fails: no cortisol (↑ACTH), no aldosterone (salt-wasting), no testosterone (XY underdeveloped genitalia), no DHEA/androstenedione (very low), no progesterone, no estradiol. 17-OHP is LOW because 17-OH-pregnenolone cannot be made (substrate is missing before the 17α-hydroxylase step).

Key distinction: 21-OH deficiency = adrenal only, allows some gonadal function. CYP11A1 = total steroidogenic failure in ALL tissues. This is a "global steroidogenesis blackout," not a pathway traffic jam.

Lipoid CAH: Caused by StAR mutation (cholesterol trapped in mitochondria), same clinical phenotype as CYP11A1 deficiency.
Integrates L2 (adrenal steroidogenesis, CYP11A1 as the rate-limiting step) with L3 (gonadal steroidogenesis). Tests whether you know the first committed enzyme blocks EVERYTHING downstream.
Vignette 3L3 + L4 — Sex Steroids + ADH/Osmolyte Control
A 24-year-old woman with 46,XY karyotype, inguinal testes, absent uterus, and normal female external genitalia is brought to the ER with symptomatic hyponatremia (Na = 126). She receives large-volume IV fluids. After fluid correction to Na = 135, she develops central pontine myelinolysis.
→ What is her diagnosis? Why did she develop hyponatremia from IV fluids? What went wrong with the fluid correction?
✅ Answer
Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS). 46,XY with functional androgen receptor deficiency → testes present (produce testosterone, MIF), female phenotype (external genitalia), no uterus (MIF suppresses Müllerian structures).

Hyponatremia mechanism: AIS patients often have SIADH (inappropriately concentrated urine despite low serum osmolality — mechanism not fully clear, possibly from pituitary ADH oversecretion). When she received large-volume IV fluids, free water was retained → dilutional hyponatremia.

Osmotic demyelination (CPM): Brain adapts to chronic hyponatremia by losing intracellular osmolytes (K+, amino acids). When sodium is corrected RAPIDLY (Na jumped 9 mEq/L in short time), water leaves the brain cells → myelin sheath damage → delayed neurologic symptoms (dysarthria, dysphagia, weakness appear 2–6 days after correction).

Prevention: Correct hyponatremia slowly (≤8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours) even in symptomatic patients.
L3 (AIS phenotype) + L4 (osmotic demyelination syndrome, hyponatremia correction). Tests integrated thinking: recognize AIS, explain SIADH, manage hyponatremia safely.
Vignette 4L2 + L4 — Adrenal (briefly) + ADH Signaling
A 35-year-old man on lithium for bipolar disorder develops polyuria and polydipsia. ADH level is normal. Urine osmolality is LOW (100 mOsm/kg). Serum osmolality is HIGH (300). Water deprivation test: urine does not concentrate. Desmopressin injection: urine STILL does not concentrate.
→ What type of DI? Explain the V2 receptor pathway and how lithium disrupts it. What are treatment options?
✅ Answer
Nephrogenic DI (lithium-induced). The problem is in the kidney tubule (V2 receptor signaling), NOT ADH production.

Mechanism: Lithium enters principal cells via ENaC → inhibits adenylyl cyclase → ↓cAMP → ↓PKA → AQP-2 water channels not inserted into luminal membrane → free water loss despite elevated ADH/desmopressin.

Why desmopressin fails: V2 (Gs-coupled) normally: V2 → Gs → ↑cAMP → PKA → AQP-2 exocytosis. Lithium blocks the downstream signaling (↓cAMP/PKA), so even a strong V2 agonist (DDAVP) cannot restore water reabsorption. The receptor is present but the signal transduction is broken.

Treatments:
• Stop lithium if possible (may take weeks for DI to resolve)
• Amiloride (ENaC blocker → prevents lithium entry into cells)
• Thiazide diuretics (mild volume depletion → ↑proximal Na/water reabsorption → less fluid delivered to collecting duct)
• NSAIDs (↓prostaglandin synthesis → ↓polyuria)
• Ensure adequate hydration (thirst mechanism is usually intact).
Integrates L4 (V2/Gs/cAMP, AQP-2 insertion) with L2 (adrenal mention is minimal but context of lithium as a psychiatric drug). Depth: tests whether you understand tubular unresponsiveness vs. central DI.
Vignette 5L1 + L4 — Hypothalamic-Pituitary Anatomy + ADH Release
A child undergoes emergency surgery for a craniopharyngioma compressing the pituitary stalk. Post-op day 1: polyuria (central DI). Day 3: paradoxical water retention, confusion, hyponatremia (SIADH-like picture). Day 10: permanent DI.
→ Explain the triphasic response. Why does SIADH occur in the middle phase? Where are ADH and oxytocin made, and how does stalk compression disrupt their release?
✅ Answer
Triphasic DI response to pituitary stalk injury:

Phase 1 (immediate, day 1): Axonal transection → loss of ADH transport down the stalk → immediate DI (polyuria, high serum Na).

Phase 2 (days 3–5): Axonal degeneration releases stored ADH from dying axons → massive, unregulated ADH dumping → inappropriate antidiuresis (SIADH phenotype) → water retention → hyponatremia + confusion (seizures if severe).

Phase 3 (days 7–10, permanent): Stored ADH depleted, hypothalamic cell bodies die → permanent central DI (if damage is extensive) or gradual recovery (if partial damage).

ADH/Oxytocin synthesis & transport: Both made in hypothalamic nuclei (supraoptic + paraventricular) → transported down axons to posterior pituitary nerve terminals → stored in neurosecretory granules → released with electrical stimulation. Stalk compression = blocks axonal transport = prevents hormone delivery to terminals = functional posterior pituitary failure.

Why oxytocin is less affected: Oxytocin has faster axonal transport; ADH appears more vulnerable to stalk compression initially.
Pure L1 (hypothalamic anatomy, axonal transport) + L4 (posterior pituitary physiology, ADH release mechanism). Triphasic DI is a Step 1 favorite for testing pituitary anatomy.
Vignette 6L5 — Thyroid Pharmacology
A 68-year-old woman with Graves disease undergoes emergency appendectomy (surgical stress). Post-op: heart rate 140, temperature 104°F, confusion. Thyroid storm diagnosed. What treatment order?
→ List the treatment sequence and explain why iodine CANNOT be given before PTU.
✅ Answer (THE most tested sequence on Step 1)
Thyroid storm treatment order:

1. β-BLOCKER FIRST (Propranolol) — blocks sympathetic effects (tachycardia, tremor, heat production, anxiety). ALSO inhibits peripheral T4→T3 conversion (↓deiodinase activity). Typically propranolol 40–80 mg q6h IV/PO.

2. PTU (Propylthiouracil), 1 hour LATER — inhibits TPO (thyroid hormone synthesis) AND blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion (unique to PTU, not methimazole). Dose: 500–1000 mg loading, then 250 mg q4–6h.

3. IODINE (Lugol's or SSKI), 1 HOUR AFTER PTU START — high-dose iodine triggers Wolff-Chaikoff effect (high iodine transiently blocks thyroid hormone synthesis + reduces thyroid vascularity). Dose: 10 drops SSKI (Saturated Solution Potassium Iodide) q6–8h OR 1 g IV sodium iodide q6h.

⚠️ WHY this order matters: If you give iodine FIRST (before PTU blocks synthesis), the thyroid gland will use that iodine as substrate to make MORE T4/T3 → massive hormone flooding (Jod-Basedow effect) → life-threatening thyroid storm surge. PTU MUST block synthesis before you add iodine substrate. The 1-hour gap is the most tested detail on Step 1.

4. GLUCOCORTICOIDS (Hydrocortisone 50–100 mg q6h IV) — prevents adrenal crisis (concurrent adrenal insufficiency in severe thyroidism) + further blocks T4→T3 conversion. Start after β-blocker on board.

Adjunctive: Supportive care (fluids, cooling blankets, ICU monitoring).
L5 (thyroid pharmacology, drug sequencing, Wolff-Chaikoff effect). This is the classic Step 1 "sequence" question. Do NOT memorize it as trivia — understand the MECHANISM (why order matters).
Vignette 7L2 + L5 — Adrenal Tumors + Medullary Thyroid CA
A 48-year-old woman has a 5 cm adrenal mass, hypertension, elevated urine metanephrines (3× normal). Her sister has medullary thyroid cancer. She is scheduled for tumor resection next week. Pre-op assessment notes: "PTU started for presumed hyperthyroidism."
→ What is the primary diagnosis? What genetic syndrome? Why is PTU potentially dangerous? What MUST be done before surgery?
✅ Answer
Pheochromocytoma in the setting of MEN 2A (or 2B). Medullary thyroid cancer in the family → RET proto-oncogene mutation → automatic screening for pheo + hyperparathyroidism.

Why PTU is dangerous: PTU can cause severe hepatotoxicity + agranulocytosis. More importantly, PTU doesn't address the catecholamine storm from the pheo. If the sister has medullary thyroid CA (MTC) as part of MEN2, the patient may also have MTC (needs calcitonin screening). Don't distract with thyroid drugs if the adrenal crisis is imminent.

CRITICAL Step 1 detail — Pre-operative Pheo Management:
1. α-BLOCKADE FIRST (phenoxybenzamine, irreversible; OR prazosin, competitive) for 10–14 days. Dose titrated to control HTN + symptom relief. Goal: prevent hypertensive crisis during tumor manipulation.
2. β-BLOCKER ONLY AFTER α-blockade is established (propranolol or metoprolol). Never give β-blocker alone first — unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation → severe, uncontrollable hypertension. Always α first, then β.
3. Then surgery (after 10–14 days of α-blockade to allow vascular depletion of catecholamines).

The reason: intra-operative tumor manipulation releases catecholamines → ↑HR, ↑BP (sometimes to 250/150+, risk of stroke/MI). α-blockade vasodilates vessels + desaturates catecholamine stores. β-blocker added after to prevent reflex tachycardia (α-blockade alone causes reflex tachycardia).
Integrates L2 (pheo, adrenal medulla, catecholamine physiology, MEN2 genetics) with L5 (medullary thyroid CA, RET). Tests pre-operative readiness — do you know the drug sequence and WHY it matters?
Vignette 8L1 + L3 — Nuclear Receptors + Sex Steroids
A postmenopausal woman on tamoxifen for ER+ breast cancer (2 years) develops vaginal bleeding. Endometrial biopsy: hyperplasia. Her bone density is ABOVE normal for her age. Lab: TSH normal, free T4 normal.
→ Explain how the same drug (tamoxifen) causes opposite effects in uterus (bad) and bone (good). What is the mechanism of tissue-specific action?
✅ Answer
Tamoxifen = SERM (Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulator).

Tissue-specific action explained: Tamoxifen is NOT a pure ER antagonist or agonist. Its effect depends on local coactivator/corepressor proteins in each tissue:

• Breast (ER antagonist): Tamoxifen-ER complex recruits corepressors → blocks estrogen-responsive gene transcription → anti-proliferative effect on breast cancer.

• Uterus (ER agonist): Tamoxifen-ER complex recruits coactivators → activates estrogen-responsive genes → endometrial cell proliferation → hyperplasia/cancer risk (major side effect, requires surveillance with annual ultrasound or any abnormal bleeding).

• Bone (ER agonist): Tamoxifen-ER complex activates bone-protective genes → preserves bone density (beneficial effect in postmenopausal women, but must weigh against uterine risk).

Comparison: Raloxifene (alternative SERM) = ER antagonist in breast AND uterus (no endometrial risk) but agonist in bone. May be preferred if endometrial protection is priority.

L1 mechanism: Nuclear receptor action depends on coactivator recruitment — same receptor, different tissue coactivators → different gene expression outcome. This is a fundamental principle of nuclear receptor pharmacology.
L1 (nuclear receptors, coactivators, tissue-specific gene transcription) + L3 (estrogen receptor, SERM mechanism, sex steroid pharmacology). Tests whether you understand nuclear receptor biology beyond just "hormone binds = gene turns on."
Vignette 9L2 + L3 — Adrenal Enzyme Deficiency + Reproductive Endocrinology
A 28-year-old woman with infertility has hyperandrogenemia, LH:FSH >2:1, insulin resistance, polycystic ovaries. ACTH stimulation test shows 17-OHP elevated AFTER stimulation (baseline 17-OHP normal). Regular menstruation but oligoovulation.
→ What is the primary diagnosis? What secondary diagnosis must be ruled out? How do you differentiate? What are treatment approaches?
✅ Answer
Primary diagnosis: PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome). Hyperandrogenism + oligo-ovulation + polycystic ovaries on imaging. High LH (GnRH pulse frequency too high) stimulates theca cells → excess androgens. Insulin resistance → ↑LH → feeds androgen production.

Secondary diagnosis to rule out: Non-classical 21-hydroxylase deficiency (non-classical CAH / attenuated CAH). Mild 21-OH deficiency presents with similar hyperandrogenemia + irregular periods. Differentiation: ACTH stimulation test → 17-OHP rises markedly (>2 ng/mL post-stim = abnormal). In pure PCOS, 17-OHP stays normal even after ACTH stim.

Treatment approaches:
PCOS:
• Lifestyle modification (weight loss, exercise) — improves insulin sensitivity, LH/FSH ratio, and ovulation.
• Metformin (insulin sensitizer) — improves ovulation rates, reduces androgen production.
• Clomiphene citrate (ER antagonist at pituitary) — mimics low estrogen state → ↑FSH/LH surge → ovulation induction. First-line for fertility in PCOS.
• Spironolactone (androgen receptor antagonist + aldosterone antagonist) — treats hirsutism/acne from high androgens.

Non-classical CAH (if confirmed by ACTH stim test):
• Low-dose glucocorticoids (dexamethasone 0.25–0.5 mg daily or hydrocortisone) — suppresses ACTH → reduces 17-OHP + androgen synthesis.
Integrates L2 (21-OH enzyme, CAH, ACTH stimulation test) with L3 (LH/FSH, PCOS pathophysiology, androgen receptor pharmacology, clomiphene mechanism). Tests differential diagnosis thinking and treatment rationale.
Vignette 10L3 + L4 — Sex Steroids + Pituitary Anatomy
A woman delivered 4 hours ago, hemorrhaged (required massive transfusion), went into shock. Six months later: cannot breastfeed, amenorrhea, cold intolerance, weight gain. Prolactin UNDETECTABLE. Free T4 low, TSH low.
→ What is the diagnosis? Why is prolactin the FIRST hormone to fail? Why does TSH also become low (central hypothyroidism)? Explain the hormone loss cascade.
✅ Answer
Sheehan syndrome = postpartum panhypopituitarism from pituitary ischemia/infarction.

Pathophysiology: The pituitary gland enlarges 2× during pregnancy (from lactotroph hyperplasia due to high estrogen). This enlarged gland is highly vascularized but at the mercy of uterine blood flow. Massive hemorrhage → severe hypotension → pituitary ischemia → cell death (anterior lobe is most vulnerable; posterior lobe usually spared).

Why prolactin fails FIRST: Prolactin is the most sensitive to ischemia (anterior pituitary cells are the most metabolically active; lactotrophs are especially sensitive to oxygen deprivation). Inability to breastfeed (no milk letdown from oxytocin failure, no milk production from prolactin failure) is the FIRST clinical symptom (often noticed at day 1–2 postpartum).

Why TSH becomes low (central hypothyroidism): TRH from hypothalamus normally stimulates pituitary thyrotrophs → ↑TSH → thyroid makes T4/T3. Pituitary ischemia → thyrotroph death → ↓TSH → low T4 (secondary/central hypothyroidism). This manifests as cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue (not immediately but over weeks/months).

Hormone loss cascade (order):
1. Prolactin (most sensitive, lost by day 1–2)
2. GH + LH/FSH (lost over days 3–7) → amenorrhea
3. TSH (lost over weeks) → hypothyroidism
4. ACTH (lost last, but when lost = adrenal crisis) → hypotension + hyponatremia

L3 connection: Amenorrhea = ↓LH/FSH → no ovarian stimulation → low estrogen (secondary hypogonadism). This perpetuates low TSH because estrogen normally enhances TRH sensitivity at the pituitary.

Clinical pearl: Any postpartum woman who cannot breastfeed (low/no milk production) should be evaluated for Sheehan syndrome, especially if there was significant hemorrhage.
L4 (pituitary anatomy, hormone loss cascade, posterior pituitary oxytocin for milk letdown) + L3 (FSH/LH control of menstrual cycle, estrogen effects on pituitary). Integrates physiology with clinical recognition.
