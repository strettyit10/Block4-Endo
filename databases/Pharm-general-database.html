Big
Metformin (Glucophage) First-line T2DM
Biguanide
AMPK → ↓hepatic gluconeogenesis No hypoglycemia GI side effects Lactic acidosis if eGFR <30
▾
Mechanism
Activates AMPK → primarily ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis (main effect). Also: ↓ intestinal glucose absorption, ↑ peripheral insulin sensitivity, mild ↓ fatty acid oxidation. Does NOT stimulate insulin secretion.
Indications
First-line for T2DM (per ADA/USMLE). Pre-diabetes (if high-risk). PCOS (off-label).
Side effects
GI: nausea, diarrhea, metallic taste (take with food, titrate slowly). Lactic acidosis (rare but fatal — black box warning). No hypoglycemia (no insulin secretion). Weight neutral to modest ↓. B12 deficiency (chronic use).
Contraindications
eGFR <30 (lactic acidosis risk). Reduce dose if eGFR 30-45. Hold before IV contrast and 48h after (renal protection). Hepatic failure. Alcoholism.
CV benefit
UKPDS trial: ↓ cardiovascular events in obese T2DM — one of few DM drugs with proven macrovascular benefit.
Metformin causes lactic acidosis if it accumulates (impaired renal clearance). Hold before contrast procedures. The GI side effects are the most common reason for discontinuation — start low, titrate slowly, take with food. It does NOT cause hypoglycemia — if a patient on metformin alone presents with hypoglycemia, look for another cause.
USMLE loves asking: which DM drug is safe to use in CKD stage 3 but not stage 4-5? Metformin is safe if eGFR >30-45, but contraindicated if eGFR <30. Which drug doesn't cause hypoglycemia? Metformin (+ GLP-1i, SGLT2i, DPP-4i, TZDs).
SU
Sulfonylureas Glipizide, Glyburide, Glimepiride
Insulin secretagogues (require functional β-cells)
Close K-ATP channel → insulin release Hypoglycemia Weight gain Glyburide dangerous in elderly/CKD
▾
Mechanism
Bind SUR1 subunit of K-ATP channel on β-cell membrane → close K-ATP channel → membrane depolarizes → Ca²⁺ influx → insulin granule exocytosis. Glucose-INDEPENDENT → releases insulin even when glucose is normal → hypoglycemia risk.
Drugs (know these)
1st gen: Tolbutamide, Chlorpropamide (not commonly used). 2nd gen (Step 1): Glipizide (Glucotrol) — preferred in elderly; Glyburide (Micronase) — active metabolites accumulate in CKD → prolonged hypoglycemia; Glimepiride — once daily.
Side effects
Hypoglycemia (major — especially glyburide in elderly/CKD). Weight gain (↑ insulin). Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol (chlorpropamide). Hyponatremia (chlorpropamide — enhances ADH). Agranulocytosis (rare).
Require
Functional β-cells — ineffective in T1DM or end-stage T2DM with β-cell exhaustion.
Glyburide in CKD → active metabolites not cleared → prolonged severe hypoglycemia. Use glipizide instead (hepatically metabolized to inactive products). BOTH sulfonylureas cause hypoglycemia — they are glucose-independent insulin releasers.
Classic Step 1 Q: "Patient on sulfonylurea + recently lost 30 lbs + increased exercise → hypoglycemia." Answer: Exercise + weight loss → ↑ insulin sensitivity → less drug needed → now excessive insulin release → hypoglycemia. Solution: reduce/stop sulfonylurea.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists CV benefit in ASCVD Weight loss
Incretin mimetics (injectable + oral semaglutide)
Glucose-dependent insulin secretion No solo hypoglycemia Weight loss 3–15 kg Nausea (main SE) Personal/family hx MTC
▾
Drugs (Step 1)
Exenatide (Byetta — twice daily), Liraglutide (Victoza — daily SC), Semaglutide (Ozempic SC weekly; Rybelsus — oral), Dulaglutide (Trulicity — weekly).
Mechanism
Bind GLP-1 receptor on β-cells → glucose-DEPENDENT insulin secretion (only when glucose is elevated). Also: ↓ glucagon from α-cells, ↓ gastric emptying (→ satiety), act on hypothalamus → satiety → weight loss.
CV benefit
LEADER (liraglutide), SUSTAIN-6 (semaglutide), REWIND (dulaglutide): ↓ MACE (MI, stroke, CV death) in patients with established ASCVD. Add to metformin in T2DM + ASCVD.
Side effects
Nausea, vomiting (worst initially, improves). Pancreatitis (rare, controversial — discontinue if pancreatitis occurs). Thyroid C-cell tumors (seen in rodents with liraglutide/semaglutide — black box warning).
Contraindications
Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or MEN2. Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. History of pancreatitis (relative).
No hypoglycemia in isolation — glucose-dependent. If combined with insulin/sulfonylurea → hypoglycemia risk increases. GLP-1 agonists ≠ DPP-4 inhibitors: GLP-1 agonists are injectable (mostly), much more potent, cause weight loss. DPP-4i are oral, weight neutral, modest effect.
Semaglutide (Ozempic) is FDA-approved for weight loss at higher doses (Wegovy). The first oral GLP-1 agonist is oral semaglutide (Rybelsus). Both achieve weight loss approaching bariatric surgery in trials.
SGL
SGLT2 Inhibitors HF benefit CKD benefit
Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (renal)
Block renal glucose reabsorption → glucosuria ↓ HF hospitalizations ↓ CKD progression UTI / genital mycotic infections Euglycemic DKA
▾
Drug (Step 1)
Canagliflozin (Invokana), Empagliflozin (Jardiance), Dapagliflozin (Farxiga).
Mechanism
Block SGLT2 in the proximal renal tubule → prevent glucose reabsorption → glucosuria → lower blood glucose. Also cause natriuresis → ↓ preload → HF benefit. ↓ glomerular hyperfiltration → renal protection.
Benefits beyond glucose
HF: EMPA-REG, DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced — ↓ HF hospitalizations (add to T2DM + HFrEF). CKD: CREDENCE — ↓ CKD progression. BP: modest ↓. Weight: ↓ 2-4 kg.
Side effects
UTI and genital mycotic infections (glucosuria → infection). Fournier's gangrene (rare but serious — necrotizing fasciitis of perineum). Euglycemic DKA (glucose can be normal!). ↑ risk of lower limb amputations (canagliflozin — FDA warning). ↑ fractures (canagliflozin).
Renal dosing
Stop for glycemic effect if eGFR <45. Can continue for HF/CKD benefit down to eGFR ~20. Acute small eGFR dip after starting = expected and protective — do NOT stop.
Euglycemic DKA: SGLT2i can cause DKA with a normal or mildly elevated glucose. Check ketones (not just glucose) in sick patients on SGLT2i. Hold SGLT2i before major surgery, hospitalization, or prolonged fasting. The acute eGFR dip after starting is protective — do NOT stop the drug for this alone.
DPP
DPP-4 Inhibitors "-gliptins" Weight neutral
Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (gliptins)
↑ endogenous GLP-1 half-life Glucose-dependent → no solo hypoglycemia Nasopharyngitis, headache Saxagliptin ↑ HF hospitalizations
▾
Drug (Step 1)
Sitagliptin (Januvia), Saxagliptin (Onglyza), Alogliptin, Linagliptin.
Mechanism
Block DPP-4 enzyme → prevents degradation of endogenous GLP-1 and GIP (incretins) → ↑ incretin levels → glucose-dependent insulin secretion + ↓ glucagon. Modest A1c reduction (~0.5-0.8%).
Side effects
Nasopharyngitis (most common). Headache. Pancreatitis (rare). Saxagliptin: ↑ HF hospitalizations (SAVOR-TIMI) → avoid in HFrEF. Sitagliptin and alogliptin: CV neutral.
Renal dosing
Most require dose reduction in CKD. Sitagliptin: 100 mg/day if normal; 50 mg if CrCl 30-50; 25 mg if CrCl <30. Linagliptin does NOT require renal dose adjustment (excreted in bile).
Saxagliptin is the specific gliptin associated with ↑ HF hospitalizations (SAVOR-TIMI trial). Prefer sitagliptin or alogliptin if patient has HF. DPP-4i ≠ GLP-1 agonists: DPP-4i work indirectly by prolonging endogenous GLP-1; GLP-1 agonists are exogenous GLP-1 analogs — much more potent, cause weight loss, injectable.
TZD
Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) Pioglitazone, Rosiglitazone
PPARγ agonists — insulin sensitizers
PPARγ → ↑ insulin sensitivity Weight gain, fluid retention, edema HFrEF (CONTRAINDICATED) Bladder cancer risk (pioglitazone)
▾
Drug (Step 1)
Pioglitazone (Actos) — preferred. Rosiglitazone (Avandia) — restricted use (↑ CV risk concerns).
Mechanism
Activate PPARγ (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ) → transcription of insulin-responsive genes → ↑ insulin sensitivity in adipose, muscle, liver. Requires insulin to be present to work (sensitizer, not secretagogue).
Side effects
Fluid retention + edema (PPARγ increases renal Na+ reabsorption). Weight gain (fat redistribution + fluid). Worsens HF (fluid overload). Bone fractures (↑ risk in women — PPARγ shifts MSC differentiation from osteoblasts to adipocytes). Bladder cancer (pioglitazone — FDA warning). Rosiglitazone: ↑ LDL/TC.
Contraindicated
Heart failure (HFrEF) — worsens fluid retention → decompensation. Active or history of bladder cancer (pioglitazone).
TZDs are CONTRAINDICATED in heart failure — this is a high-yield Step 1 contraindication. If a T2DM patient has HFrEF, use SGLT2i instead (which has proven HF benefit). TZDs have SLOW onset — 2-4 weeks for full effect (gene transcription).

Other DM Agents — Acarbose, Pramlintide, Meglitinides
Alpha-glucosidase inhibitor · Amylin analog · Insulin secretagogues
Various mechanismsGI side effects (acarbose)
▾
Acarbose (Precose)
Alpha-glucosidase inhibitor. Blocks intestinal alpha-glucosidase → delays carbohydrate digestion → blunts postprandial glucose spike. Taken WITH meals. GI side effects (flatulence, diarrhea) — limits use. No hypoglycemia (doesn't affect insulin). If hypoglycemia occurs while on acarbose + sulfonylurea: treat with GLUCOSE (not sucrose — sucrose digestion is blocked).
Pramlintide (Symlin)
Amylin analog. Synthetic amylin (co-secreted with insulin by β-cells) → ↓ glucagon, ↓ gastric emptying, ↑ satiety. Given with insulin in T1DM and T2DM on insulin. Mild weight loss. Risk of severe hypoglycemia when combined with insulin (must reduce insulin dose). N/V common.
Meglitinides / Glinides
Nateglinide, Repaglinide. Also close K-ATP channel (same as sulfonylureas but bind different site). Shorter duration → taken WITH each meal. Hypoglycemia (less than sulfonylureas — shorter action). Weight gain. Require functional β-cells.
Acarbose hypoglycemia trap: If patient on acarbose + sulfonylurea develops hypoglycemia, treat with glucose tablets/gel or IV dextrose — NOT orange juice or sucrose (acarbose blocks sucrase too). Pure glucose must bypass the blocked enzyme.

Thyroid Pharmacology
Replacement therapy, antithyroid agents, and radioiodine. High-yield: PTU vs methimazole in pregnancy is a classic Step 1 discriminator.
HypothyroidismHyperthyroidismPregnancy Traps
T4
Levothyroxine (Synthroid, Levoxyl) First-line hypothyroidism
Synthetic T4 (thyroxine) — thyroid hormone replacement
T4 prohormone → peripheral conversion to T3 Stable levelsExcess → iatrogenic hyperthyroid
▾
Mechanism
Provides T4 (prohormone) → peripheral tissues convert T4 → T3 (active) via 5'-deiodinase. T4 has t½ ~7 days → once daily dosing → stable, predictable levels. Liothyronine (T3) has t½ ~1.5 days, more side effects — rarely used.
Monitoring
Titrate to normal TSH (TSH is the most sensitive monitor). In thyroid cancer post-thyroidectomy: TSH-suppressive doses used (keep TSH below normal) to prevent residual cancer stimulation.
Drug interactions
Absorption reduced by: calcium, iron, antacids, PPIs, cholestyramine (take on empty stomach, 4h apart from these). Metabolism increased by: rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine (enzyme inducers → may need higher dose).
Overdose
Iatrogenic hyperthyroidism: palpitations, weight loss, heat intolerance, osteoporosis (chronic), atrial fibrillation. Monitor TSH — do not suppress unnecessarily.
Post-thyroidectomy for thyroid cancer: deliberately suppress TSH (high-dose LT4) → TSH <0.1 → removes trophic stimulus for any residual thyroid cancer cells. This is therapeutic, not inadvertent.
PTU
PTU & Methimazole Pregnancy trap
Thionamides — antithyroid agents
Block TPO → block organification PTU 1st trimester; Methimazole 2nd/3rd Agranulocytosis (both) PTU: hepatotoxicity (black box)
▾
Shared mechanism
Both block thyroperoxidase (TPO) → inhibit iodide oxidation, organification, and coupling of MIT/DIT → ↓ TH synthesis. Both are thionamides. Both require existing TH stores to deplete before effect seen (weeks).
PTU unique
ALSO blocks peripheral 5'-deiodinase → inhibits T4→T3 conversion. This makes PTU preferred in thyroid storm (rapidly reduces active T3). Black box warning: hepatotoxicity (rare but severe liver necrosis). Shorter t½ → multiple daily doses.
Methimazole advantages
Longer t½ → once daily dosing. Preferred for most hyperthyroid patients (except 1st trimester pregnancy and thyroid storm). Side effects are dose-related (reduce dose if side effects).
Pregnancy rule
1st trimester: PTU (methimazole causes VACTERL defects, aplasia cutis, choanal atresia → teratogenic). 2nd/3rd trimester: methimazole (PTU hepatotoxicity risk outweighs benefit). PTU is also preferred in breastfeeding and thyroid storm.
Side effects (both)
Rash, pruritus, arthralgias (dose-related). Agranulocytosis (most serious, ~0.5% — monitor for fever/sore throat → CBC → stop drug). Hepatitis (both, but PTU more severe). GI symptoms.
Pregnancy drug of choice: PTU for 1st trimester, switch to methimazole for 2nd/3rd. I-131 is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy (ablates fetal thyroid). Agranulocytosis is the most dangerous side effect — any fever/sore throat → check WBC immediately.
Quick Check
A 32-year-old woman with Graves' disease is 8 weeks pregnant. Which antithyroid medication is most appropriate?
I131
Radioiodine (I-131) + Lugol's Iodine
Ablative therapy + pre-surgical iodine loading
β-radiation destroys thyroid follicular cells Hypothyroidism (expected outcome) Contraindicated in pregnancy / breastfeeding
▾
I-131 mechanism
Radioactive iodine is concentrated by thyroid cells via NIS → emits β-radiation locally → destroys follicular cells → ↓ TH production. Most definitive non-surgical treatment for Graves' and toxic nodules. Most patients become hypothyroid and require lifelong levothyroxine.
Contraindications
Pregnancy (ablates fetal thyroid). Breastfeeding. Active thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy — may worsen). Use with caution in women of childbearing age (delay conception 6 months).
Lugol's iodine
High-dose iodine given pre-thyroidectomy (Wolff-Chaikoff effect): transiently blocks organification → ↓ TH synthesis + ↓ thyroid vascularity → reduces intraoperative bleeding. Also used in thyroid storm (give AFTER PTU — if given first, iodine may be used to make more TH).
Beta-blockers in hyperthyroid
Propranolol (beta-blocker, non-selective) is used adjunctively in hyperthyroidism to control symptoms (palpitations, tremor, anxiety, heat intolerance). Does NOT treat the underlying cause. Propranolol also partially inhibits T4→T3 conversion at high doses.
In thyroid storm, the correct order is: PTU first (block new synthesis + T4→T3 conversion) → THEN iodine (Wolff-Chaikoff) → beta-blocker → hydrocortisone (blocks T4→T3 + adrenal reserve). Never give iodine first — it can paradoxically provide substrate for more TH if TPO is still active.

Addison's replacement
Hydrocortisone 15-20mg/day (split dosing, AM-heavy). PLUS fludrocortisone 0.1mg/day (for mineralocorticoid). Stress dosing: 100mg IV q8h for major surgery/illness.
Dexamethasone uses
1) Dexamethasone suppression test (Cushing's workup). 2) Cerebral edema (tumor/abscess — ↓ VEGF). 3) Bacterial meningitis (before or with antibiotics → ↓ inflammatory response). 4) Nausea/vomiting. 5) Croup. No mineralocorticoid activity → safe to use without causing fluid retention in testing.
Chronic GC side effects
Cushing's features: central obesity, muscle wasting, poor wound healing, skin thinning, osteoporosis, hypertension, hyperglycemia, immunosuppression (↓ T cells, neutrophil demargination). HPA axis suppression → adrenal atrophy → cannot respond to stress → adrenal crisis if suddenly stopped.
Never abruptly stop chronic steroids — adrenal cortex has atrophied (no ACTH stimulation for weeks-months) → cannot produce cortisol for stress response → adrenal crisis. Dexamethasone has NO mineralocorticoid activity — it cannot replace fludrocortisone in Addison's. Also, dexamethasone doesn't cross-react with most cortisol assays → useful for testing even when patient is on dexamethasone.
Flu
Fludrocortisone (Florinef) Mineralocorticoid replacement
Synthetic mineralocorticoid — aldosterone replacement
MR agonist → Na+ retention, K+ excretion Addison's disease (with hydrocortisone) Hypertension, edema, hypokalemia if overdosed
▾
Mechanism
Activates mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) in renal collecting duct → ↑ Na+ reabsorption + ↑ K+ and H+ secretion → volume expansion + blood pressure support. Required in primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's) because adrenal cortex cannot make aldosterone.
Use
Given orally 0.1 mg/day combined with hydrocortisone in Addison's. Also used in: salt-wasting CAH, orthostatic hypotension.
Hydrocortisone alone is NOT sufficient for Addison's — at physiological doses, it has insufficient mineralocorticoid activity to replace aldosterone. Both hydrocortisone (GC) + fludrocortisone (MC) are required. Dexamethasone has ZERO MC activity — never substitute.

Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists — Spironolactone, Eplerenone
Aldosterone antagonists — for primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's)

Mechanism
Competitively bind mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) → block aldosterone → ↓ Na+ reabsorption → ↓ K+ excretion → natriuresis + hyperkalemia risk.
Spironolactone
Non-selective MR antagonist. Also blocks androgen receptors and progesterone receptors → gynecomastia in men, menstrual irregularities in women. Also used in HFrEF (mortality benefit), ascites/cirrhosis, PCOS, acne (anti-androgen).
Eplerenone
More selective MR antagonist (no anti-androgen/anti-progestin activity) → no gynecomastia. Also used in HFrEF (EPHESUS trial). More expensive.
Key uses
Primary hyperaldosteronism (bilateral idiopathic hyperplasia — when surgery not done). HTN. HFrEF (both spironolactone and eplerenone ↓ mortality in NYHA III-IV). Ascites. CAH (salt-wasting, adjunct).
Hyperkalemia is the major risk — especially dangerous if combined with ACEi/ARBs/K+ supplements (triple whammy). Monitor K+ closely. Spironolactone causes gynecomastia/breast pain in men (anti-androgen). Switch to eplerenone if problematic.
Pheo
Pheochromocytoma Management — Alpha then Beta
Pre-operative pharmacological preparation — sequence matters critically
Alpha FIRST, then beta Phenoxybenzamine: irreversible α-blocker 10–14 days pre-op prep
▾
Step 1: Alpha-block
Phenoxybenzamine (irreversible, non-selective α1 + α2 blocker) — given 10-14 days pre-op. Blocks the α1-mediated vasoconstriction from catecholamine surge during tumor manipulation. Doxazosin (selective α1, reversible) is an alternative, preferred in pregnancy and some centers.
Step 2: Beta-block
After alpha-blockade is established → add beta-blocker (propranolol) to control reflex tachycardia from α1 blockade. NEVER give beta-blocker first → removes β2-mediated vasodilation → unopposed α1 → severe hypertensive crisis.
Intraoperative
IV phentolamine (reversible alpha-blocker) or sodium nitroprusside for acute hypertensive crises during tumor manipulation. Nicardipine (CCB) increasingly used.
Also high-volume saline
High-salt diet + IV fluids during prep to expand volume (contracted from chronic vasoconstriction). Prevents severe hypotension post-resection.
Beta before alpha = potentially fatal. Beta-blockers remove β2-mediated vasodilation → unopposed α1 → HTN crisis. This is the most classic Step 1 drug sequence question for pheo. The same logic applies: avoid TCAs (block NE reuptake → amplifies NE), MAOi (block catecholamine degradation → NE accumulates), and sympathomimetics (pseudoephedrine, ephedrine) in pheo.

Santi · Watson — Pituitary Pharmacology
Pituitary Pharmacology
Dopamine agonists are first-line for prolactinoma. Somatostatin analogs for acromegaly. Adrenal enzyme inhibitors for Cushing's Disease.
ProlactinomaAcromegalyCushing's Disease
DA
Dopamine Agonists — Cabergoline, Bromocriptine First-line prolactinoma
D2 receptor agonists — suppress prolactin, shrink lactotroph adenomas
D2 agonist → ↓ prolactin → tumor shrinkage Prolactinoma (even macro) N/V, orthostatic hypotension Cabergoline: cardiac valvulopathy (high dose)
▾
Mechanism
Activate D2 receptors on lactotroph cells → inhibit prolactin synthesis and secretion. Also cause physical tumor shrinkage (unique among pituitary drugs — surgery not always needed).
Cabergoline
D2-selective, long-acting (twice weekly dosing). Preferred over bromocriptine — better tolerated, more effective tumor shrinkage. Side effects: N/V, headache, dizziness; cardiac valvulopathy at high doses (Parkinson's doses — not at prolactinoma doses).
Bromocriptine
Non-selective dopamine agonist. Older, more side effects (more N/V), shorter acting (multiple daily doses). Preferred in pregnancy (more safety data). Also used in: acromegaly (modestly), T2DM (Cycloset — Dopamine-2 agonist that improves insulin sensitivity).
When to use
First-line for ALL prolactinomas — including macroadenomas. Surgery is reserved for: drug intolerance/resistance, rapid vision loss, chiasm compression not improving on drug.
Stalk compression trap: mild prolactin elevation (<150 ng/mL) + large sellar mass → may be stalk compression (dopamine delivery blocked) NOT a prolactinoma. A true prolactinoma causing a 2+ cm mass → prolactin usually >500 ng/mL. Giving cabergoline to a craniopharyngioma won't shrink it → delayed diagnosis → vision loss.
SS
Somatostatin Analogs — Octreotide, Lanreotide, Pasireotide
Somatostatin receptor agonists — suppress GH, TSH, glucagon, insulin
Mimic SS → ↓ GH/IGF-1 Acromegaly (post-op residual disease) TSH-secreting adenoma GI: N/V/D, cholelithiasis Pasireotide → hyperglycemia
▾
Mechanism
Bind somatostatin receptors (SSTR1-5) → inhibit GH secretion from somatotrophs, ↓ IGF-1, inhibit TSH (thyrotrophs), suppress gut hormones (glucagon, insulin, gastrin, secretin). Suppress GH by 50-70% in acromegaly.
Octreotide/Lanreotide
Long-acting depot formulations (monthly injections) for acromegaly maintenance. Also used in: carcinoid syndrome (↓ VIP/serotonin → flushing/diarrhea), VIPoma, glucagonoma, esophageal variceal bleeding (↓ portal pressure).
Pasireotide
Multi-receptor SS analog (SSTR 1,2,3,5) → used in Cushing's Disease (inhibits ACTH from corticotrophs). Also used in acromegaly. Significant hyperglycemia (blocks insulin secretion) — requires close glucose monitoring.
Side effects
GI: N/V, diarrhea, abdominal cramps (reduce by injecting at mealtime). Cholelithiasis (impairs gallbladder emptying — monitor with US). Hyperglycemia (especially pasireotide — blocks insulin). Bradycardia (mild).
GH
GH Axis Drugs — Pegvisomant, Somatropin, Sermorelin
GH receptor antagonist · Recombinant GH · GHRH analog
Pegvisomant: blocks GH receptor → ↓ IGF-1 Acromegaly (resistant to SS analogs) Somatropin: GH deficiency replacement
▾
Pegvisomant
GH receptor antagonist (competitive) → blocks GH signaling → ↓ IGF-1. Used in acromegaly resistant/intolerant to somatostatin analogs. Note: GH levels RISE (no negative feedback removal — blocks at receptor, not synthesis). Tumor may enlarge without SS analog (no direct pituitary effect). Monitor IGF-1 (not GH) for treatment success.
Somatropin (rGH)
Recombinant human GH — used for GH deficiency (children: stunted growth; adults: body composition, lipids, bone density). Can cause glucose intolerance (GH is diabetogenic). Monitor IGF-1 during treatment. Contraindicated in active malignancy.

Posterior Pituitary & DI Drugs
DDAVP is the cornerstone of DI treatment. Understanding why it differs from natural AVP is key to distinguishing central vs. nephrogenic DI.
AVP

Why DDAVP ≠ AVP
1) D-arginine at position 8 → V2-selective (no V1a vasoconstriction → safe for antidiuresis). 2) Deaminated N-terminus → resistant to vasopressinase → works in gestational DI. 3) Longer half-life. 4) Can be given intranasal or oral (rapid absorption, avoids first-pass metabolism).
Central DI
Cannot make biologically active AVP → kidney V2R intact → DDAVP works → urine concentrates. Treat: intranasal or oral DDAVP.
Nephrogenic DI
V2R or AQP2 defect → DDAVP fails. Treat: thiazide diuretics (paradoxical ↓ urine output — volume contraction → ↑ proximal reabsorption → less to distal tubule), low-Na diet, NSAIDs (indomethacin — for lithium-induced).
Gestational DI
Placental vasopressinase degrades natural AVP → DDAVP resistant → works. Treat: DDAVP throughout pregnancy; resolves after delivery.
Other uses
Hemophilia A and vWD: DDAVP releases stored vWF and Factor VIII from endothelium (V2R on endothelial cells → stored vWF exocytosis). Also: nocturnal enuresis, mild hemostatic issues before procedures.
Oxytocin
Synthetic oxytocin (Pitocin) used clinically to: induce/augment labor (IV infusion), treat postpartum hemorrhage (IM after delivery → uterine contraction). SE: water intoxication (MR cross-reactivity at high doses → hyponatremia), hypotension.
Thiazides in nephrogenic DI — paradoxical mechanism: block NaCl reabsorption in DCT → mild volume depletion → ↑ proximal tubule reabsorption (angiotensin II–mediated) → less fluid reaches collecting duct (AVP-dependent segment) → net ↓ urine output. A diuretic that reduces urination. Classic Step 1 paradox question.
