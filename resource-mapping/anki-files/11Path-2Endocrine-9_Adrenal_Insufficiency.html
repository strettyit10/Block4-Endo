1.1
Tertiary adrenal insufficiency is seen in patients with chronic exogenous [...] use, precipitated by [...]

  
Tertiary adrenal insufficiency is seen in patients with chronic exogenous glucocorticoid use, precipitated by abrupt withdrawal

- Chronic exogenous steroids leads to a chronic suppression of ACTH release, leading to adrenal atrophy over time; thus discontinuing steroids abruptly leads to hypoadrenalism → tertiary from treatment

- Therefore chronic adrenal insufficiency patients who go for surgery are given a single dose of high dose steroids (stress dose steroids) to prevent insufficiency; also the basis for weaning patients off of steroids (slow discontinuation)

- Aldosterone synthesis is unaffected

In primary adrenal insufficiency, the metyrapone stimulation test results in increased ACTH and unchanged 11-deoxycortisol

- Administration → ↓ cortisol → loss of negative feedback on anterior pituitary → ↑ ACTH secretion

- Normal is increased ACTH and 11-deoxycortisol

- Metyrapone blocks conversion of 11-deoxycortisol → cortisol

   Adrenal insufficiency may cause adrenal crisis following a stressful event (e.g., surgery, infection), characterized by hypotensive shock, confusion, fever, and abdominal pain

*bonus: what is the next step in management?
- *Immediate glucocorticoids (e.g., hydrocortisone or dexamethasone); dexamethasone is often preferred because it does not interfere with plasma cortisol level that may be needed to confirm the diagnosis

   What initial tests should be performed in a patient with suspected adrenal insufficiency?

1. Morning serum cortisol
2. Morning plasma ACTH
3. Cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test

- Low cortisol with high ACTH confirms primary adrenal insufficiency

- Cosyntropin (~ ACTH) stimulation test should normally increase cortisol, but this does not happen
